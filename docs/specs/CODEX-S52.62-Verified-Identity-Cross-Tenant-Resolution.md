# CODEX S52.62 — Verified Identity & Cross-Tenant Person Resolution (Draft v1)

**Status:** Draft for red-team — not yet built. Research-grounded (see §7 for what was actually verified in code, not assumed).

**Origin:** Grew out of CODEX S52.61 ("Add Client Across Suites"). While mapping how a client's Contacts record and Company Vault footprint should stay in sync across the business-in-a-box suites, Sean asked the deeper question underneath it: when the *same real person* shows up in more than one business (a nursing student who is also a tenant who is also a vet client — this platform's own Sara Kahele demo persona spans exactly these three), does the platform know they're the same person, or does it silently create disconnected, potentially-duplicate records the way Salesforce/SAP/Azure-based CRM systems do? This codex is the answer to that question, plus what it would take to make the answer "yes, and provably so" instead of "no."

---

## 1. The claim Sean wants to be able to make

In a conventional relational-database CRM/ERP, identity is a bundle of static, stealable facts (SSN, phone number, a password) — if someone gets those, they can plausibly impersonate you everywhere that record is trusted. Sean's target claim for SOCIII: because identity here is anchored to a real biometric ID-verification pass (Stripe Identity — face + government ID match), compromising it would require something categorically harder than phishing a password — stealing someone's actual face and ID.

**This claim is not yet true today, and the reason why is precise, not vague:** the identity-verification webhook currently discards Stripe's verified output entirely (see §3). It records that a face-and-ID check happened, but not *what* it verified. A "we anchor identity to biometric proof" claim requires that proof to actually be retained as data — right now there's a pass/fail flag and nothing else. Closing that gap is the first, load-bearing step in this codex, not an optional enhancement to it.

**Precise language matters here beyond honesty — it's a real liability surface.** SOCIII itself never touches biometric data (the face scan) — that stays entirely with Stripe. What SOCIII would store, per §4, is a keyed HMAC of Stripe's *derived text* output (name, DOB, document number), not biometric data at all. That is actually a *better* regulatory position than "we store biometric identifiers" — but only if the claim is worded that way. Loose language like "anchored to biometric proof" or "stealing your face and ID" risks pulling this claim under biometric-privacy statutes (Illinois BIPA and similar frameworks in other states) that define "biometric identifier" broadly enough to invite exactly the scrutiny this precise architecture doesn't warrant. **Recommended external-facing language, pending legal review:** "verification is anchored to a one-time biometric check performed by our verification provider; SOCIII itself stores only a keyed cryptographic hash of the verified result, never biometric data." Do not use the looser framing publicly until legal has reviewed it.

Two distinct guarantees are being blended together in the natural way this claim gets pitched, and they should stay separate in how it's actually said publicly:
- **Harder to open a fraudulent identity / claim to be someone you're not** — this is what biometric KYC actually buys you, and with §3's fix, SOCIII could claim it honestly.
- **Harder to take over an existing account** — this depends on ongoing session/auth security (passwords, tokens, 2FA), which biometric KYC at signup does not protect by itself unless verification is re-required at sensitive moments. Don't claim this one without a separate, deliberate design decision about when re-verification is required.

Anchored/append-only records (the Deposition Rule, independent anchoring — see CLAUDE.md and the platform's existing audit-trail work) is a third, related but distinct guarantee: it protects against undetected tampering *after* a record exists. It's a real strength worth naming, just not the same claim as identity verification itself.

## 1.5. Scoping resolution: this mechanism is for natural persons, not organizations — ~~proposed~~ **Decided (2026-08-22, Sean): correct as written below.**

This entire codex is a **person**-identity mechanism: a biometric check ties a real human's face and government ID to an HMAC. That has no meaning for a company. Voltara BV, as an organization, cannot undergo a biometric check — only a human representative acting on its behalf can. This creates a real, previously-unaddressed scoping conflict with CODEX S52.61: S52.61 §4.3 names DPP's actual business-model target as "a manufacturer's own production/ERP system creates new SKUs (and potentially onboards itself as a client) directly, with no consultant in the loop at all" — and describes similar future automation for MLS-triggered title clients and loan-origination-triggered MSR borrowers. None of those system-to-system flows have a human present to biometrically verify. **The vertical S52.61 names as its flagship automation target is structurally the one case this codex's mechanism can never reach.** Two codexes whose worked examples don't actually intersect is a real problem, not a footnote.

**Resolution, decided:**

- **This codex's cross-tenant resolution mechanism (§4) applies only to natural-person clients** who individually pass biometric KYC — a nursing student, a borrower, a tenant, a vet client, a brokerage's individual buyer.
- **Organizational/B2B clients (a manufacturer, potentially a lending institution) are structurally out of scope for this mechanism.** If SOCIII wants deduplication or verification for company-level clients, that needs its own, separate mechanism (e.g., business-registration/EIN-based verification) — not a variant of the biometric HMAC approach, and not designed here.
- **A company onboarding still likely has one human moment worth capturing:** even "full automation" for DPP plausibly still has an authorized human representative sign the initial master agreement (S52.61 §5's e-signature step). That signer *could* individually pass KYC as a person, establishing their own `verifiedIdentityId` — but that identifies the *signer*, not the company, and doesn't extend to every subsequent SKU the ERP creates afterward with no human in the loop.
- **"The manufacturer's ERP onboards itself as a new client with zero human ever" (the literal phrase in S52.61 §4.3) is the sharpest edge case and is not resolved by any of the above.** If that flow is genuinely intended — a brand-new B2B relationship forming with no human touchpoint at all — it is inherently a lower-trust, unverified relationship by this codex's own standard, and that should be stated as a deliberate, accepted tradeoff (with whatever compensating controls a B2B integration would normally carry — API-key issuance, a contractual relationship established through some other channel) rather than silently assumed to be covered by "add client."

Until this is resolved explicitly, S52.61 §7's Phase 1 (reserve `verifiedIdentityId` on the Contacts schema) should be understood as applying to natural-person Contacts rows specifically — not a blanket field on every kind of client record S52.61 describes.

## 2. The Sara Kahele test case — what's actually there today

**Terminology note, added per third red-team pass:** "tenant" in this document and its companion (S52.61) always means the SaaS sense — a business/customer of SOCIII (a nursing school, a vet clinic, a landlord's own business account) — never a literal rental tenant. Sara Kahele's own worked example below is confusingly also a rental tenant in the plain-English sense, which is exactly the kind of collision that gets lost once this spec turns into code comments, field names, and conversations with people (Elise, Ruthie, Mike) who aren't tracking SaaS jargon. A rental tenant, in this codebase, is a Contacts record — not a "tenant" in the multi-tenancy sense. Worth keeping this distinction explicit anywhere this codex gets referenced going forward.

Sara Kahele is the platform's own existing demo persona spanning nursing-student, university-tenant (a lease at "123 Any Street"), and vet-client (owner of a dog named Kui at Meadow Creek Veterinary Clinic) roles — a naturally-occurring test case for exactly this problem, already sitting in the codebase.

**Verified via direct code and a live Firestore query:** all three (four, counting a UH variant) of her personas are tied together by nothing more than a hardcoded shared Firebase uid (`sara-kahele-demo`) in the demo PERSONAS map (`functions/functions/index.js`). The cross-org provisioning for her vet and landlord relationships (`SARA_EXTRA_ORGS`) only writes `memberships` and `users/{uid}/workspaces/{tenantId}` docs — no Contacts record, no identity-verification record, no durable link of any kind.

A direct query of the live `contacts` collection (11,869 documents) for any name/text match on "Kahele" returned **zero results**, in any of the three businesses. The "same person across three businesses" story that this demo tells is a shared-uid artifact of the demo-persona system, not a real mechanism. If Sara Kahele were a real, independent person who happened to sign up separately at a nursing school, a landlord, and a vet clinic, **nothing in the platform today would know she's the same person** — three disconnected Contacts rows (if Contacts were even wired to create them, which per S52.61 it currently isn't) would be the realistic outcome. This is precisely the Salesforce problem Sean named, confirmed to exist here today, not hypothetically.

## 3. Identity verification infrastructure — real, but shallow and tenant-siloed

A genuine, working Stripe Identity KYC flow exists: `/v1/identity:session:create` and a webhook handler at `/stripe:webhook` (`functions/functions/index.js`, roughly lines 1654-1737 and 24530-24581).

Two hard limits, both confirmed directly in the webhook code:

1. **It only stores pass/fail, not the verified identity itself.** The document ID is built as `identityDocId({uid, tenantId, purpose})` and the record stores `stripeStatus` (verified/pending/canceled) and `stripeSessionId`. Stripe's `verified_outputs` — the actual verified legal name, date of birth, document number — are never read from the webhook payload or persisted anywhere. The system today can answer "did this uid pass a check for this tenant and purpose" but not "who, provably, did it verify."
2. **It's deliberately tenant-scoped, with no cross-tenant concept.** A code comment reads, verbatim, "STRIPE IDENTITY (scoped by tenant)." `users/{uid}.identity[purpose]` aggregates verification status across tenants, but only for the *same* Firebase uid — two different Firebase accounts (e.g., two different emails) belonging to the same real person get zero linkage, by design.

Contacts and identityVerifications share no fields today — a direct check of the `/contacts:add` handler found no reference to any verification record at all.

**What this means:** the foundation (a real biometric check, a real per-tenant Contacts system) is reasonable, but it was built entirely for single-tenant KYC gating. Cross-business person-matching is new design work, not a matter of connecting two things that already do most of the job.

## 4. What a real fix needs, architecturally

1. **Decided (2026-08-22, Sean): key a keyed HMAC of the verified output — not a plain hash, and not the raw biometric data.** When the Stripe Identity webhook fires, extract `verified_outputs` (legal name, DOB, document number — whatever Stripe returns) and compute `HMAC(serverSecretKey, normalized name + DOB + document number)`, storing only the HMAC output. **A naive unkeyed hash is a real vulnerability, not a simplification to fix later:** DOB has only ~36,500 possible values and document numbers follow known per-country formats, so a deterministic hash with no secret key is dictionary/rainbow-table attackable — anyone with a target's name and DOB could confirm a match against the database without ever breaching it, and an attacker could precompute matches at scale. An HMAC with a properly access-controlled, server-side-only secret closes this; a plain hash does not. This needs a real cryptography/security review before implementation, not just "hash it" treated as solved. This HMAC output — not the Firebase uid, which resets every time someone signs up again — is the durable "this is provably the same real person" key. It is the prerequisite everything else depends on; it doesn't exist today.
2. **A cross-tenant Person/Identity record**, structurally separate from both per-tenant Contacts rows and per-tenant Firebase Auth accounts, keyed by that verified-identity HMAC.
3. **Per-tenant Contacts carry a `verifiedIdentityId` field**, populated only once that specific contact has independently passed KYC in that tenant. Two Contacts rows (one at the vet, one at the nursing school) with matching `verifiedIdentityId` values is what proves — not suggests — they're the same person.
4. **Only verified contacts get linked.** An unverified Contacts row stays an ordinary, standalone CRM entry. This is deliberate: the dedup key is cryptographic proof of a real identity check, never fuzzy name/email matching, which is exactly the failure mode this codex exists to avoid.
5. **Strict data-isolation boundary, even across linked identities.** Proving "the vet's Sara and the nursing school's Sara are the same real person" must never mean the vet clinic can see the nursing school's data about her, or vice versa. The link is a resolvable identity key, not a data merge. Each tenant's Contacts row stays exactly as private as it is today; only the *fact of sameness* is knowable, and only to systems/roles that should have any business seeing it (an important open question for §5).
6. **Match failures will happen, in both directions, and neither is silent-safe.** Exact HMAC matching on name+DOB+document-number will fail to match the same real person if Stripe's OCR reads a middle initial differently, a hyphenated name differently, or a passport vs. a driver's license formats a field differently — producing two different `verifiedIdentityId` values for one real person with no signal to anyone that resolution failed. This is a known, documented limitation of the exact-match approach, not a solved lookup problem — §1's claim should be read with this caveat attached, not as an unqualified guarantee.
7. **Verification narrows fraud, it doesn't eliminate it.** Because verification unlocks real benefits (cross-tenant resolution, presumably faster/more-trusted onboarding), fraud-prone actors are the ones most incentivized to *stay* unverified and route around this entirely. This system reduces the population of exploitable identities to the unverified segment — it does not close the loop on fraud generally.
8. **Identity drift breaks the key silently, with no reconciliation path — added per second red-team pass.** The HMAC in item 1 is derived from legal name + DOB + document number. Real people legally change names (marriage, divorce, gender transition) and renew ID documents with new document numbers, both routinely. When that happens, re-verification produces a *different* HMAC, silently breaking the cross-tenant link — with no alert to anyone, and no way for the person to say "these are still me." This isn't a rare edge case; it's a nontrivial fraction of real users over any multi-year horizon. This needs either a documented, accepted limitation or a reconciliation path (e.g., a supervised re-linking flow where a person can attest that a new verification supersedes an old one, itself logged as its own audit event) — right now it's neither raised as an open question elsewhere nor solved, and should not ship silently as "the hash is a stable key," because it isn't, for a meaningful number of people.
9. **Hash-algorithm versioning — a future-self problem being created right now if unaddressed.** If the HMAC construction changes for any reason (including fixing the exact salting/keying approach per item 1), every previously-computed identity link breaks with no migration plan. The `verifiedIdentityId` scheme should carry a version tag from day one so a future algorithm change can be reasoned about, rather than becoming an undocumented mass-breakage event discovered later.
10. **Decided (2026-08-22, Sean): anchor type is an explicit decision, not silent inheritance of a default.** This platform has a real, existing Record Anchors abstraction (`raas/RECORD_ANCHORS.md`) supporting multiple anchor types per entity — `database` (the stated default for all entities), `hash` (on- or off-chain), `blockchain`/`nft`, `notary`, `escrow`, `registry` — anchoring is not automatically blockchain, contrary to what might be assumed. But neither this codex nor S52.61 states which anchor type the Person/Identity record would get, and that silence is the actual risk: if this record follows a "durable, trust-critical records get blockchain anchoring" instinct by default, it collides directly with item 3/§5.3's retention-and-deletion requirement — a blockchain/NFT anchor is immutable by design, and a GDPR/CCPA erasure request cannot be honored against a permanent public-adjacent ledger the way it can against a database row. Permanence also compounds the crackability risk from item 1: a moderately weak HMAC sitting in a database is a contained risk; the same HMAC sitting in a permanent, timestamped, unerasable ledger gives an attacker unlimited time for an offline attack with no way to ever rotate the value out. **Decided:** the verified-identity HMAC and the Person/Identity record must use the `database` (or off-chain `hash`) anchor type, never `blockchain`/`nft` — this needs to be a stated exemption from whatever default anchoring instinct applies to other durable platform records, not an assumption anyone building this discovers only after the anchoring code is written the standard way. The existing `status: active | released | expired | superseded` field on the anchor model may partially serve identity-drift reconciliation (§ item 8) by marking a superseded HMAC rather than deleting it — but note a "superseded" flag alone likely does not satisfy a genuine right-to-erasure request, which probably requires actual removal; whether the Record Anchors abstraction's append-only model can accommodate real deletion for this specific record type is itself an open question needing legal and architecture input before build, not just before anchoring is wired up.
11. **Data-layer isolation is currently described as a promise, not a structural guarantee.** Item 5's isolation boundary ("must never mean the vet clinic can see the nursing school's data") is stated as design intent, but the architecture in item 2 puts the cross-tenant Person/Identity record in a single structure separate from per-tenant Contacts rows. That means isolation lives entirely in the application/API layer today — whoever has direct database or admin query access to that shared structure can see every cross-tenant link at once, bypassing every tenant boundary simultaneously. This is a materially larger risk than §5 item 1's question of which *roles get a UI* to see linkage. Needs its own explicit answer: who has raw data-layer access to the shared identity structure, is that access itself logged/audited, and is tenant isolation enforced anywhere below the API layer (e.g., row-level security) or only above it.
12. **The cross-tenant claim's strength is capped by Stripe's own accuracy, not stronger than it — a distinct and arguably worse failure mode than identity drift (item 8).** Item 8 covers the same real person legitimately getting two different hashes over time. The unaddressed inverse: if someone commits identity theft and passes Stripe KYC using a genuinely stolen identity (someone else's real legal name, DOB, and document number), the resulting HMAC will correctly match the *victim's* real `verifiedIdentityId` — the hash construction cannot distinguish "the real person" from "someone holding the real person's stolen documents." The system would then confidently report these as the same real person, potentially extending the fraudulent account whatever cross-tenant trust or visibility the linkage implies. This matters more here than it would generically, because §1's claim explicitly positions this mechanism as *harder to fake than a password* — but the honest ceiling is that SOCIII's cross-tenant identity claim can never be stronger than Stripe's own upstream document-forgery/theft false-acceptance rate. §1's claim language should acknowledge this ceiling explicitly rather than implying an accuracy guarantee stronger than its own upstream dependency provides.
13. **No internationalization treatment, despite the platform actively onboarding EU users today.** DPP/Volta Advisory is explicitly EU-facing (GS1, Battery Regulation). Document number formats, name-ordering conventions (given-name/family-name order varies by country), and date formats (DD/MM vs. MM/DD) all vary internationally. If item 1's normalization step handles these inconsistently — e.g., an OCR/parsing pipeline that assumes US MM/DD/YYYY formatting for a European document — the same real person could get different hashes depending on which document type or locale was used at verification, silently breaking cross-tenant matching specifically for the users this platform is actively onboarding right now, not a hypothetical future user base. Not addressed anywhere in either document; needs explicit locale-normalization rules before the hash construction is implemented, not discovered after EU verifications start producing unlinked records.
14. **No monitoring/observability plan for when any of the above fail silently.** Across all four review passes, several real failure modes were identified (identity drift, OCR/locale mismatch, fraud-collision, partial onboarding, hash-algorithm changes). None of this codex proposes any way to *detect* these in production. At minimum this needs: a metric for the rate of Contacts rows created with no `verifiedIdentityId` after some threshold of time (proxy for stalled/abandoned KYC, tying to item 17 in S52.61's partial-onboarding-state gap), and an alert heuristic for the same normalized name+DOB combination producing two different HMACs within a short window (a proxy signal for either a construction bug or a genuine identity-drift event worth a human look). Without this, every failure mode identified across four rounds of review will fail silently in production and only be discovered via a downstream symptom — a duplicate Contacts row, a support complaint — rather than the system surfacing it proactively.

## 5. Open questions (explicit, not resolved by this codex)

1. **Who can see that two records are linked, and how?** Is cross-tenant identity resolution visible to no one (purely a backend dedup mechanism, e.g. to prevent fraud or duplicate onboarding), visible to the person themselves (a real "your SOCIII identity" view across all businesses you interact with), or selectively visible to specific platform-level functions (fraud/compliance) but not to individual tenants? This determines a lot of the actual data model and is a real product/privacy decision, not an engineering one.

   **The binary framing above understates the risk — added per second red-team pass.** §4.5's "only the fact of sameness is knowable, never the data itself" is still a real leak, not a null result, at any visibility setting above "invisible to all tenants." Simply telling Tenant A that a contact's identity resolves to *some* record elsewhere on the platform is information a sophisticated tenant can act on. Concrete case: a landlord noticing an applicant's identity resolves to multiple simultaneous contexts consistent with a nursing-school-adjacent tenant population — without ever seeing nursing-school data directly — could still infer "probably a student," which carries real fair-housing/tenant-screening implications if that inference shapes a leasing decision. This risk exists at any visibility level short of fully invisible, and needs to be weighed as its own cost when deciding item 1's answer — not treated as a solved problem because no raw data crosses the boundary.
2. **Where does re-verification fit?** If SOCIII wants to claim "hard to take over an existing account," not just "hard to fake a new identity," some sensitive actions (large fund transfers, changing bank details, etc.) may need to re-require a biometric check rather than relying on the original one-time KYC pass. Not scoped here — flagged as a real design question the security claim in §1 depends on.
3. **Retention and deletion — Decided (2026-08-22, Sean): interim default accepted, standing real legal review as a condition of going live with real user PII, not blocking further design/build.** A verified-identity HMAC is deterministic over low-entropy inputs (name, DOB, document number) — very likely still "personal data" under GDPR/CCPA-style frameworks, not anonymized data. **Decided interim default:** a deletion request removes the HMAC from the record and marks it superseded, retaining only an anonymized "a deletion occurred on this date" audit entry with no reconstructible PII — consistent with §4 item 10's `database`/off-chain-`hash` anchoring decision, which makes this kind of real removal possible in the first place. This default is sufficient to build against now; it still needs real legal confirmation before this system holds actual user PII in production, and that confirmation is a standing condition to check off before launch, not a question blocking the work between now and then.
4. **Does this need to be opt-in per business?** Some tenants (e.g., a business explicitly not wanting cross-tenant visibility into their clients at all, for competitive or contractual reasons) may want to opt out of contributing to or benefiting from cross-tenant resolution. Not decided.
5. **Cost model — added per third red-team pass, not previously addressed in either document.** Stripe Identity checks are not free (roughly $1.50+ per check depending on volume/tier), and pricing conversations elsewhere have already priced in a real per-check cost for the pilot vertical. If KYC becomes a default step in "add client" across every vertical (per §1.5's scoping and S52.61 §3's still-open KYC-trigger decision), plus §5 item 2's proposed re-verification at sensitive moments, that cost compounds — and it plausibly does not pencil out uniformly. A high-volume, low-margin vertical (education enrollment at scale is the obvious example) absorbing a per-check cost that's entirely reasonable for MSR borrowers or DPP manufacturer relationships is a real business-model question, not just an implementation detail. Needs a cost pass before Phase 1 defaults KYC-on-every-add anywhere.
6. **Whether Stripe actually returns the fields §4.1 depends on — flagged as an assumption, not a verified fact, unlike everything in §7's list.** §4.1's fix assumes Stripe's `verified_outputs` reliably contains legal name + DOB + document number for whatever verification session type SOCIII currently configures. Stripe's Identity product returns different fields depending on session configuration (document-only vs. document+selfie vs. id-number-only checks) — this hasn't been checked against Stripe's actual dashboard/API for the specific session type this platform uses. Worth a direct check before scoping the build, so the fix isn't designed around fields that turn out sparse or absent for the configured session type.
7. **Namespace/scheme marker for `verifiedIdentityId`, extending the versioning item in §4.9.** If a different verification method is ever needed for cases where biometric KYC is impractical (per §1.5, organizational clients would need a wholly different scheme if this is ever extended to them), a bare nullable field can't distinguish "never verified" from "verified via a different method" from "verified via Stripe HMAC v1." The field should carry a scheme/method tag alongside the version tag from day one, not just a version number for the one method currently planned.

## 6.5. Acceptance criteria — added per third red-team pass, since neither document previously stated one

Concrete test that would demonstrate this mechanism actually works, scoped to natural-person clients per §1.5:

1. Create three independent real (or real-shaped test) accounts with matching legal name, DOB, and document number, onboarded separately through three different tenants (e.g., a nursing school, a landlord, and a vet clinic) — mirroring the Sara Kahele case exactly.
2. Confirm all three produce an identical `verifiedIdentityId`.
3. Confirm each tenant's own Contacts data about that person remains fully isolated from the other two tenants — only the matching identifier should be shared, nothing else (per §4.5's isolation boundary).
4. Create a fourth account with a different legal name/DOB/document number and confirm it receives a distinct `verifiedIdentityId`, not a false match.
5. (Once §5 item 6 above is resolved) confirm the same real person re-verifying after a legal name or document change either reconciles to the same identity through whatever mechanism §4.8 lands on, or is documented as a known, accepted limitation if no reconciliation path is built.

Without a stated target like this, red-teaming the eventual build has nothing fixed to check against.

## 6. Relationship to CODEX S52.61 and the Gmail/Drive/Calendar bug

This codex and S52.61 (Add Client Across Suites) are complementary, not competing: S52.61 is about the *workflow* of onboarding a client (manual click or automated trigger) and granting portal access; this codex is about what makes the resulting Contacts record *trustworthy* as the same real person across businesses, rather than a fresh, disconnected guess every time. S52.61's Phase 1 (shared onboarding capability) should be built with this codex's `verifiedIdentityId` field in mind from the start, even if the cross-tenant resolution logic itself lands later.

The Gmail/Drive/Calendar tenant-scoping bug found during this same investigation (integration tokens keyed only by Firebase uid, with a code comment confirming the cross-tenant sharing was deliberate — "available to ALL workers when the user has connected Google Drive") is the **opposite** problem: data too loosely shared across tenants that should be isolated, versus this codex's problem of tenants too strictly isolated to recognize a real, provable same-person match. Both are real, but they are not the same bug and don't share a fix.

**Status: fixed and deployed (updated after this codex's red-team pass; the prior draft correctly declined to claim this before it was confirmed).** Tokens moved to `users/{uid}/workspaces/{tenantId}/integrations/{provider}` — reusing the existing per-(user,tenant) doc shape already used elsewhere in this codebase, not a new one. Every call site (chat-tool execution for search/send/read across Gmail, Drive, and Calendar, plus two additional stale call sites found only by grepping every caller of the changed functions — Alex's calendar-context prompt builder and the 15-minute email-queue cron) was updated to pass tenantId through. Migration for Sean's existing real connection: the first workspace where he uses Gmail/Drive/Calendar after this deploy transparently keeps working (old doc copied forward, never deleted); every other workspace correctly shows "not connected" and requires an explicit fresh Connect — that's the fix working as intended, not a regression. `node -c` clean on all 9 touched backend files, frontend build clean, `firebase deploy --only functions:api,hosting` completed successfully. **Not verified:** live OAuth behavior (deliberately not tested against Sean's real account) and live cross-workspace isolation with a demo account (blocked by a sensitive-data access guard; verification relied on code-level review only). One known, documented limitation: `driveAuth.js`'s Drive client accepts `tenantId` as optional so existing untouched callers (document generation, e-sign, Drive import) keep working unchanged on the legacy path — those were left out of scope deliberately, not missed.

## 8. Red-team findings, consolidated and tiered (covers both CODEX S52.61 and this document)

Four review passes surfaced over 25 individual findings across the two documents. Read as a flat list, that reads as 25 equally-urgent problems, which isn't true and isn't useful for turning this into a Phase 1 ticket. This section is the answer to "what do we actually have to resolve before starting, versus what's a documented decision versus what's a one-line acknowledgment" — ranked, not just collected.

**Tier 1 — CLOSED (2026-08-22, Sean's decisions recorded inline throughout both documents):**

1. ~~KYC-trigger gap and the B2B scoping mismatch.~~ **Decided:** cross-tenant resolution applies to human-verified, natural-person clients only (§1.5); B2B/system-to-system automation (DPP's manufacturer ERP, future MLS/loan-origination triggers) is explicitly out of scope for this mechanism. KYC is required at "add client" for MSR and DPP (the manufacturer's signer specifically), optional/staff-discretion elsewhere (CODEX S52.61 §3).
