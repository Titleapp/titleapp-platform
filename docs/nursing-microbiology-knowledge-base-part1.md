### 1.1 What Our Ancestors Knew

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
1. "Describe how our ancestors improved food with the use of invisible microbes"
2. "Describe how the causes of sickness and disease were explained in ancient times, prior to the invention of the microscope"
3. "Describe key historical events associated with the birth of microbiology"

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Microorganism / Microbe**: Very small organisms, many too small to see without magnification, though some parasites and fungi are visible to the naked eye.

**Microbial Fermentation**: Process using bacteria, mold, or yeast to convert sugars into alcohol, gases, and organic acids.

**Gram Stain**: A procedure differentiating between gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria.

**Lumbar Puncture (Spinal Tap)**: Procedure extracting cerebrospinal fluid samples from around the spinal cord for laboratory analysis.

#### Core Content

**Ancient Food Production**: Archaeological evidence shows fermented beverages and foods (beer, wine, bread, yogurt, cheese, pickled vegetables) date back millennia. A Neolithic Chinese site revealed fermented beverage from rice, honey, and fruit as early as 7000 BC.

**Early Disease Understanding**:
- **Ötzi the Iceman** (5,300 years old) showed evidence of attempting treatments using fungal materials with antibiotic properties.
- **Indus Valley Civilization** (2500 BCE): Complex networks of wells, baths, and drainage systems.
- **Ancient Rome**: Aqueducts and the Cloaca Maxima sewer system.

**Foundational Thinkers**:
- **Hippocrates** (460–370 BC): Dismissed supernatural disease causation; proposed natural environmental or internal causes; "father of Western medicine."
- **Thucydides** (460–395 BC): Observed Athenian plague survivors showed immunity upon re-exposure.
- **Marcus Terentius Varro** (116–27 BC): Proposed invisible "minute creatures" cause disease, describing them as floating in air and entering through mouth and nose.
- **Al-Razi/Rhazes** (Islamic Persian scholar): Distinguished measles from smallpox; selected hospital locations based on meat rot rates.
- **Ibn Sina/Avicenna**: Compiled the Canon of Medicine (1025); described contagion mechanisms; advanced quarantine practices.

**Birth of Microbiology**:
- **Antonie van Leeuwenhoek** (1632–1723): Developed powerful lenses enabling observation of single-celled organisms in rainwater, called "animalcules."
- **Golden Age of Microbiology** (1857–1914):
  - **Louis Pasteur**: Demonstrated unique microbial strain properties, fermentation causation, invented pasteurization, developed vaccines.
  - **Robert Koch**: First to connect isolated microbes to specific human diseases (anthrax, cholera, tuberculosis).

#### Clinical Focus: Bacterial Meningitis
Cora, a 41-year-old with severe headaches, high fever, and neck stiffness, underwent lumbar puncture for cerebrospinal fluid analysis. Laboratory testing included visual examination, microscopy (checking cell counts), centrifugation with Gram staining, and culture growth. About 80% of bacterial meningitis patients show bacteria via Gram stain, though Cora's was negative; antibiotics were prescribed preemptively.

### 1.2 A Systematic Approach

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- Describe how microorganisms are classified and distinguished as unique species
- Compare historical and current systems of taxonomy used to classify microorganisms

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Taxonomy**: "The classification, description, identification, and naming of living organisms."

**Classification**: Organizing organisms into different groups based on shared characteristics.

**Taxonomic Hierarchy Levels**: Domain, Kingdom, Phylum, Class, Order, Family, Genus, Species — species is the most basic unit.

**Phylogenetic Trees**: Diagrams depicting evolutionary relationships between organisms, arranged by relatedness.

**Prokaryotes**: Organisms lacking membrane-bound nuclei.

**Eukaryotes**: Organisms possessing membrane-bound nuclei.

**Binomial Nomenclature**: "A two-word naming system for identifying organisms by genus and specific epithet." Names are italicized; genus capitalized, species lowercase. Example: *Homo sapiens*. Abbreviated form: *E. coli*.

**Horizontal Gene Transfer**: Process where genes transfer between species' genomes, particularly common in microorganisms.

**Three-Domain System** (Carl Woese and George Fox): Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya — based on genetic analysis of ribosomal RNA sequences.

**Bergey's Manuals**: Standard references for identifying and classifying prokaryotes, using biochemical, serological tests, and DNA/rRNA sequencing.

#### Clinical Focus
Cora's negative bacterial/fungal cultures from cerebrospinal fluid suggest viral meningitis. Progressive neurological symptoms (difficulty walking, involuntary jerking, cognitive decline) indicate potential prion disease or unusual viral infection.

### 1.3 Types of Microorganisms

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- "List the various types of microorganisms and describe their defining characteristics"
- "Give examples of different types of cellular and viral microorganisms and infectious agents"
- "Describe the similarities and differences between archaea and bacteria"
- "Provide an overview of the field of microbiology"

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Pathogens**: Disease-causing microorganisms in humans and other organisms.

**Prokaryotes**: Organisms whose cells lack a nucleus; includes Bacteria and Archaea domains.

**Eukaryotes**: Organisms whose cells contain a nucleus; includes protists, fungi, plants, animals.

**Protists**: Informal grouping of eukaryotes that are not plants, animals, or fungi.

**Algae**: Photosynthetic organisms, mostly protists, surrounded by cellulose cell walls; produce oxygen and carbohydrates.

**Protozoa**: Diverse protists forming food web backbones; some move via cilia, flagella, or pseudopods; some parasitic.

**Fungi**: Eukaryotes with chitin cell walls; not photosynthetic; includes unicellular yeasts and multicellular molds.

**Yeasts**: Unicellular fungi found in diverse environments; used beneficially (bread, fermentation) or cause spoilage/disease.

**Molds**: Multicellular fungi with visible filament colonies; decompose dead organisms; some produce mycotoxins; used for penicillin production.

**Helminths**: Multicellular parasitic worms; not technically microorganisms but studied in microbiology due to microscopic life stages.

**Viruses**: "Acellular microorganisms" containing proteins and genetic material (DNA or RNA, never both); replicate by hijacking host cells.

**Prions**: Acellular infectious agents containing abnormal proteins only; no genetic material; cause transmissible spongiform encephalopathies.

#### Size and Scale
- An object must measure about 100 micrometers (µm) to be visible without a microscope.
- Typical animal cells: ~10 µm
- Bacterial cells: ~1 µm
- Viruses: ~10 times smaller than bacteria

#### Prokaryotic Microorganisms

**Bacteria**:
- Found in nearly every habitat; mostly harmless or beneficial; some are pathogens.
- Genetic material (DNA) not housed in nucleus.
- Most contain peptidoglycan in cell walls.
- Common shapes: spherical (coccus), rod-shaped (bacillus), curved (spirillum, spirochete, vibrio).
- Wide metabolic range; some photosynthetic.

**Archaea**:
- Unicellular prokaryotes with different evolutionary history from bacteria.
- Cell walls contain pseudopeptidoglycan (not peptidoglycan).
- Found in extreme environments: very cold, hot, basic, or acidic conditions.
- Some inhabit human body but NONE confirmed as human pathogens.

#### Eukaryotic Microorganisms

**Protozoa**:
- Move via cilia, flagella, or pseudopods.
- Free-living or parasitic; most harmless but some pathogenic.
- Example: *Giardia lamblia* causes severe diarrhea.

**Fungi**:
- Chitin cell walls (not cellulose or peptidoglycan).
- Yeasts: Beneficial uses in baking/fermentation; can cause vaginal infections, oral thrush.
- Molds: Critical for decomposition; some produce mycotoxins; used in pharmaceutical production (penicillin, cyclosporine).

**Helminths**:
- Guinea worm (*Dracunculus medinensis*): causes dizziness, vomiting, diarrhea, painful ulcers.
- Cases dropped from 3.5 million (mid-1980s) to 126 (2014).

**Viruses**: "A virus consists of proteins and genetic material—either DNA or RNA, but never both." Inert outside host organisms. Responsible for diseases from common cold to Ebola.

#### Clinical Connection: Creutzfeldt-Jakob Disease (CJD) — Prions
- Transmissible spongiform encephalopathy caused by prions.
- ~300 cases annually in United States.
- Symptoms: rapid cognitive decline, abnormal EEG patterns.
- Transmission: exposure to infected nervous tissue, contaminated surgical equipment, infected donor tissue.
- ~85% sporadic cases with no identifiable cause.
- Fatal disease; ~90% mortality within 1 year.
- No approved treatment.

#### Microbiology Subfields
- **Bacteriology**: Study of bacteria
- **Mycology**: Study of fungi
- **Protozoology**: Study of protozoa
- **Parasitology**: Study of helminths and parasites
- **Virology**: Study of viruses
- **Immunology**: Study of immune system

#### Bioethics Note
The 1940s Guatemala syphilis study deliberately exposed 1,300+ subjects to STDs without informed consent, resulting in 83 deaths by 1953. Demonstrates the importance of ethical standards including informed consent, institutional review boards, accurate record-keeping, and confidentiality.

### 3.3 Unique Characteristics of Prokaryotic Cells

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
1. Explain the distinguishing characteristics of prokaryotic cells
2. Describe common cell morphologies and cellular arrangements typical of prokaryotic cells and explain how cells maintain their morphology
3. Describe internal and external structures of prokaryotic cells in terms of their physical structure, chemical structure, and function
4. Compare the distinguishing characteristics of bacterial and archaeal cells

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Prokaryotic Cells**: Cells lacking a nucleus surrounded by a complex nuclear membrane; generally contain a single, circular chromosome located in a nucleoid region.

**Eukaryotic Cells**: Cells with a nucleus surrounded by a complex nuclear membrane containing multiple rod-shaped chromosomes.

**Cytoplasm**: "A gel-like substance composed of water and dissolved chemicals needed for growth."

**Plasma Membrane** (also cytoplasmic membrane or cell membrane): The structure containing cytoplasm and internal cellular components; exhibits selective permeability.

**Nucleoid**: The region where prokaryotic DNA and associated proteins concentrate within the cell.

**Plasmids**: "Small, circular, double-stranded DNA molecules" found as extrachromosomal DNA in prokaryotes.

**Cell Wall**: A structure protecting cells from osmotic pressure changes; composed differently across organism types.

**Tonicity**: The degree to which a cell can withstand osmotic pressure changes.
- **Isotonic Medium**: Solute concentrations inside and outside the cell are approximately equal.
- **Hypertonic Medium**: External solute concentration exceeds internal concentration → plasmolysis (membrane pulls away from cell wall) or crenation (without cell wall).
- **Hypotonic Medium**: Internal solute concentration exceeds external → lysis (cell bursts).

**Ribosomes (70S)**: Prokaryotic protein synthesis structures composed of 30S small and 50S large subunits. (Eukaryotic ribosomes are 80S = 40S + 60S.) This difference is exploited by antibiotics.

**Inclusions**: Cytoplasmic structures storing excess nutrients in polymerized form.

**Endospores**: Dormant structures protecting bacterial genomes during unfavorable conditions.

**Sporulation**: The process by which vegetative cells transform into endospores.

**Peptidoglycan** (murein): "The major component of bacterial cell walls; unique to bacteria." Made of alternating N-acetylglucosamine (NAG) and N-acetylmuramic acid (NAM) molecules cross-linked by peptide bridges.

**Gram-Positive Bacteria**: Bacteria with thick peptidoglycan layers (30-100 nm) embedded with teichoic acids. Stain purple in Gram stain.

**Gram-Negative Bacteria**: Bacteria with thin peptidoglycan layers (~4 nm) plus a complex outer membrane containing lipopolysaccharide (LPS). Stain pink/red in Gram stain.

**Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)**: Outer membrane molecule functioning as endotoxin in gram-negative infections. Causes fever and septic shock when released.

**Capsule**: Organized polysaccharide or protein layer outside the cell wall. Aids biofilm formation; helps pathogens evade phagocytosis.

**Slime Layer**: Loosely attached layer more easily removed than capsules.

**Biofilms**: Microbe colonies forming in layers on surfaces with protective properties.

**Fimbriae**: Short bristle-like proteins projecting from cell surfaces by the hundreds; enable surface attachment. Important for pathogenic bacterial colonization.

**Pili** (singular: pilus): Longer, less numerous protein appendages aiding surface attachment. F pili (sex pili) facilitate DNA transfer between bacterial cells (conjugation).

**Flagella**: Stiff spiral filaments composed of flagellin protein; structures used for cell movement. Composed of basal body (motor), hook, and filament.

**Chemotaxis**: Purposeful movement toward chemical attractants or away from repellents.

**Mycolic Acids**: Waxy external layers in Mycobacteriaceae cell walls; characteristic of acid-fast bacteria (e.g., *M. tuberculosis*). Require special staining (Ziehl-Neelsen/acid-fast stain).

**Pseudopeptidoglycan** (pseudomurein): Peptidoglycan-like polymer in archaeal cell walls — different chemistry from bacterial peptidoglycan.

#### Common Cell Morphologies (Shapes)
- **Coccus** (cocci): Spherical/round
- **Bacillus** (bacilli): Rod-shaped
- **Vibrio**: Curved rods (comma-shaped)
- **Coccobacillus**: Short oval rods
- **Spirillum**: Long spiral/corkscrew
- **Spirochete**: Long, loose helical spiral, flexible

#### Common Cell Arrangements
- **Diplococcus**: Pair of two cocci
- **Tetrad**: Four cells in a square
- **Streptococcus**: Chain of cocci
- **Staphylococcus**: Cluster of cocci (grape-like)
- **Streptobacillus**: Chain of rods
- **Palisade**: V- or L-shaped formation of rods

#### Core Content

**Bacterial Cell Wall Structure**:

*Gram-Positive Structure*: 30-100 nm thick peptidoglycan layers embedded with teichoic acids that stabilize the structure. Mycobacteria possess external waxy mycolic acid layers requiring acid-fast staining.

*Gram-Negative Structure*: Thin peptidoglycan layers (~4 nm) with complex outer envelopes. The periplasmic space contains gel-like matrix. Outer membrane contains lipopolysaccharide (LPS). LPS = endotoxin: when released (e.g., during cell lysis or antibiotic treatment), causes fever, inflammation, septic shock.

**Archaeal Cell Walls**: Lack peptidoglycan. Instead contain pseudopeptidoglycan, glycoprotein layers, or polysaccharide layers. Some lack cell walls entirely.

**Endospores** (Clinically Critical):
Dormant structures protecting bacterial genomes during unfavorable conditions. Vegetative cells transform through asymmetric cell division. Endospores resist extreme temperatures, radiation, chemicals, and extended dormancy. When conditions improve, germination returns cells to metabolic activity.

Notable endospore-forming pathogens:
- *Bacillus anthracis* (anthrax)
- *Clostridium tetani* (tetanus)
- *Clostridium perfringens* (gas gangrene)
- *Clostridium botulinum* (botulism)
- *Clostridioides difficile* (pseudomembranous colitis / C. diff)

**Membrane Transport Mechanisms**:
- **Simple Diffusion**: Small molecules cross bilayers directly along concentration gradients without energy.
- **Facilitated Diffusion**: Carriers or channels ferry charged or large molecules down concentration gradients without energy.
- **Active Transport**: Requires ATP to move molecules against concentration gradients.
- **Group Translocation**: Molecules are chemically modified during transport.

**Flagellar Arrangements**:
- **Monotrichous**: Single flagellum at one pole
- **Amphitrichous**: Flagella at both poles
- **Lophotrichous**: Flagellar tuft at one pole
- **Peritrichous**: Flagella covering entire cell surface

Movement: Counterclockwise rotation = forward "run." Clockwise rotation = "tumble" (reorientation). Chemotaxis: when attractant detected, run lengths increase and tumble lengths decrease → net movement toward attractant.

#### Clinical Focus: Barbara's Pneumonia
Patient received amoxicillin (penicillin derivative targeting peptidoglycan) but showed incomplete recovery. Analysis: amoxicillin targets bacterial peptidoglycan cell walls. Since treatment failed, the causative agent likely either lacks peptidoglycan (viral, fungal, or *Mycoplasma*) or demonstrates amoxicillin resistance.

Resolution: *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* — prokaryotic but lacking cell walls — required azithromycin targeting bacterial ribosomes instead.

### 3.4 Unique Characteristics of Eukaryotic Cells

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- Explain distinguishing characteristics of eukaryotic cells
- Describe internal/external structures regarding physical, chemical structure, and function
- Identify organelles unique to eukaryotic cells
- Compare prokaryotic and eukaryotic cell structures
- Describe mitosis, meiosis, and contrast with binary fission

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Nucleus**: Membrane-bound structure housing the cell's DNA genome; controls cellular activities.

**Organelles**: Membrane-bound structures including mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, lysosomes, and peroxisomes.

**Cytoskeleton**: "Internal network that supports transport of intracellular components and helps maintain cell shape."

**Chromosomes**: DNA packaged in multiple linear forms (vs. prokaryotic single circular chromosome).

**Histones**: Proteins around which DNA wraps for organization and condensation.

**Nuclear Envelope**: Two distinct lipid bilayers with nuclear pores controlling material movement.

**Mitosis**: Asexual reproduction producing two identical diploid daughter cells.

**Meiosis**: Sexual reproduction creating four genetically-distinct haploid gametes.

**Nucleolus**: Dense region within the nucleus where ribosomal RNA biosynthesis occurs.

**Ribosomes**: 80S in cytoplasm; 70S in organelles (mitochondria, chloroplasts). Distinct from prokaryotic 70S — key antibiotic selectivity point.

#### Endomembrane System Components

**Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER)**:
- Rough ER: studded with ribosomes; synthesizes membrane proteins
- Smooth ER: involved in lipid biosynthesis, carbohydrate metabolism, detoxification

**Golgi Apparatus**: Modifies lipids/proteins, adds carbohydrate components producing glycolipids and glycoproteins.

**Lysosomes**: Contain digestive enzymes; break down particles and cellular debris (important in phagocytosis).

**Peroxisomes**: Produce/degrade hydrogen peroxide; involved in lipid biosynthesis.

#### Cytoskeletal Components

**Microfilaments**: Two intertwined strands of actin (6 nm diameter); enable muscle contraction and amoeboid movement.

**Intermediate Filaments**: 10 nm diameter; maintain nuclear position and anchor cells together.

**Microtubules**: Hollow tubes 23 nm in diameter composed of tubulin dimers; form mitotic spindles and organize flagella/cilia.

**Centrosomes**: Microtubule-organizing centers; composed of centrioles with triplet microtubules.

#### Major Organelles

**Mitochondria**: Site of aerobic respiration; possess double membranes, their own DNA, and 70S ribosomes (evidence of endosymbiotic origin from ancient bacteria).

**Chloroplasts**: Photosynthesis location in plant/algal cells.

#### Transport Mechanisms Unique to Eukaryotes

**Endocytosis**: Uptake of matter through plasma membrane invagination.
- Phagocytosis: "Cell eating" — engulfing large particles (e.g., bacteria by neutrophils/macrophages)
- Pinocytosis: "Cell drinking" — small dissolved materials
- Receptor-mediated endocytosis: Ligand-binding triggers uptake

**Exocytosis**: Secretory vesicles release contents externally.

#### Eukaryotic vs. Prokaryotic Flagella
**Eukaryotic flagella**: Flexible structures with "9+2 array" of microtubules with dynein motors enabling bending motion. (vs. prokaryotic flagella: rigid, rotate like propellers)

**Cilia**: Shorter than flagella; cover cell surfaces; enable feeding and mucus clearance (e.g., respiratory tract mucociliary escalator).

#### Cell Division
**Mitosis**: Produces two diploid identical daughters. Phases: prophase → prometaphase → metaphase → anaphase → telophase → cytokinesis.

**Meiosis**: Produces four haploid genetically-distinct gametes via two nuclear divisions; includes synapsis and crossing over.

#### Cell Wall Composition by Organism Type
- Plants/algae: Cellulose or silica
- Fungi: Chitin
- Bacteria: Peptidoglycan
- Archaea: Pseudopeptidoglycan (or absent)
- Animals: No cell wall; extracellular matrix (ECM) with collagen, fibronectin, proteoglycans

#### Comparative Summary Table
| Feature | Prokaryotes | Eukaryotes |
|---------|------------|-----------|
| Nucleus | No (nucleoid region) | Yes (membrane-bound) |
| Size | 1–10 µm | 5–100 µm |
| Ribosomes | 70S (30S + 50S) | 80S (40S + 60S); 70S in organelles |
| Chromosomes | Single, circular | Multiple, linear with histones |
| Cell wall | Peptidoglycan (bacteria) / Pseudopeptidoglycan (archaea) | Cellulose, chitin, or absent |
| Organelles | None (membrane-bound) | Yes (ER, Golgi, mitochondria, etc.) |
| Cell division | Binary fission | Mitosis / Meiosis |
| Flagella | Rigid, rotates; flagellin protein | Flexible, 9+2 microtubules |

### 8.1 Energy, Matter, and Enzymes

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- "Define and describe metabolism"
- "Compare and contrast autotrophs and heterotrophs"
- "Describe the importance of oxidation-reduction reactions in metabolism"
- "Describe why ATP, FAD, NAD+, and NADP+ are important in a cell"
- "Identify the structure and structural components of an enzyme"
- "Describe the differences between competitive and noncompetitive enzyme inhibitors"

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Metabolism**: "All of the chemical reactions inside a cell."

**Anabolism**: "Endergonic metabolic pathways involved in biosynthesis, converting simple molecular building blocks into more complex molecules." (Requires energy input)

**Catabolism**: "Exergonic pathways that break down complex molecules into simpler ones." (Releases energy)

**Autotrophs**: Organisms that "convert inorganic carbon dioxide (CO2) into organic carbon compounds."

**Heterotrophs**: Organisms that "rely on more complex organic carbon compounds as nutrients."

**Phototrophs**: Organisms that "get their energy for electron transfer from light."

**Chemotrophs**: Organisms that "obtain energy for electron transfer by breaking chemical bonds."

**Organotrophs**: Chemotrophs that "obtain energy from organic compounds."

**Lithotrophs**: Chemotrophs that "get energy from inorganic compounds, including hydrogen sulfide (H2S) and reduced iron."

Most bacteria that infect humans are **chemoheterotrophs** (use organic molecules for both energy and carbon).

**Redox Reactions**: Oxidation-reduction reactions involving electron transfer. Oxidation = loss of electrons; Reduction = gain of electrons. (OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain)

**NAD+/NADH**: Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide; most common mobile electron carrier used in catabolism. NAD+ is oxidized form; NADH is reduced form (carries high-energy electrons).

**NADP+/NADPH**: Variant with extra phosphate group; plays important role in anabolic reactions and photosynthesis.

**FAD/FADH₂**: Flavin adenine dinucleotide; oxidized and reduced forms extensively used in energy extraction from sugars.

**ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate)**: "Often called the 'energy currency' of the cell." Composed of adenosine + three phosphate groups. The bonds between phosphates are "high-energy phosphate bonds." Energy released when ATP → ADP + Pi (dephosphorylation).

**Enzymes**: Protein catalysts that speed up biochemical reactions by lowering activation energy. NOT consumed during reactions.

**Active Site**: Location within the enzyme where the substrate binds.

**Induced Fit**: Active-site modification in the presence of substrate, optimizing the transition state.

**Cofactors**: Inorganic ions (e.g., Fe2+, Mg2+) that help stabilize enzyme conformation.

**Coenzymes**: Organic helper molecules required for enzyme action; reusable. Many are vitamins or derived from vitamins.

**Apoenzyme**: Enzyme lacking its necessary cofactor/coenzyme; inactive.

**Holoenzyme**: Enzyme with necessary cofactor/coenzyme; active.

**Competitive Inhibitor**: Molecule similar to a substrate that competes with the substrate for binding to the active site. Example: Sulfa drugs (competitive inhibitors of bacterial folic acid synthesis).

**Noncompetitive (Allosteric) Inhibitor**: Inhibitor that binds to the enzyme at an allosteric site (location other than the active site), inducing a conformational change that reduces substrate affinity.

**Feedback Inhibition**: Use of a pathway product to regulate its own further production — a key regulatory mechanism.

#### Nursing Application
- Enzymes in pathogens are optimized for the human body temperature (~37°C); this is why fever (raising body temperature) is an innate defense mechanism — it can impair pathogen enzyme function.
- Understanding enzyme inhibition explains how antibiotics work (e.g., sulfa drugs as competitive inhibitors, beta-lactams disrupting cell wall synthesis enzymes).

### 8.2 Catabolism of Carbohydrates

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
1. "Describe why glycolysis is not oxygen dependent"
2. "Define and describe the net yield of three-carbon molecules, ATP, and NADH from glycolysis"
3. "Explain how three-carbon pyruvate molecules are converted into two-carbon acetyl groups that can be funneled into the Krebs cycle"
4. "Define and describe the net yield of CO2, GTP/ATP, FADH2, and NADH from the Krebs cycle"
5. "Explain how intermediate carbon molecules of the Krebs cycle can be used in a cell"

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Glycolysis**: The most common metabolic pathway for glucose breakdown; occurs in the cytoplasm of prokaryotic AND eukaryotic cells; does NOT require oxygen.

**Pyruvate**: Three-carbon molecule produced as the end product of glycolysis.

**Embden-Meyerhof-Parnas (EMP) pathway**: The most common glycolysis variant.

**Energy Investment Phase**: The first part of glycolysis where two ATP molecules are consumed to modify glucose.

**Energy Payoff Phase**: Second glycolysis phase — extracts energy by oxidizing G3P to pyruvate.

**Substrate-Level Phosphorylation**: The mechanism where phosphate groups from organic molecules transfer directly to ADP, producing ATP.

**Transition Reaction (Bridge Reaction)**: Converts pyruvate to a two-carbon acetyl group + CO2; produces NADH.

**Coenzyme A (CoA)**: Large carrier compound to which the two-carbon acetyl group attaches.

**Krebs Cycle** (Citric Acid Cycle / TCA Cycle): Closed metabolic loop transferring remaining electrons from acetyl groups; occurs in prokaryotic cytoplasm and eukaryotic mitochondrial matrix.

#### Glycolysis Summary (EMP Pathway)
- Input: 1 glucose (6 carbons)
- Output: 2 pyruvate (3 carbons each) + **2 net ATP** + **2 NADH**
- Occurs in cytoplasm
- Does NOT require oxygen
- Is "ancient universal metabolic process"

#### Krebs Cycle Summary (per glucose = 2 turns)
Per ONE turn of the cycle:
- 2 CO₂ released
- 1 ATP (or GTP) produced
- 3 NADH produced
- 1 FADH₂ produced

For 2 acetyl-CoA (from 1 glucose): 4 CO₂ + 2 ATP + 6 NADH + 2 FADH₂

#### Alternative Pathways
- **Entner-Doudoroff (ED) pathway**: Used by some bacteria like *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*; used to identify clinically important organisms.
- **Pentose Phosphate Pathway (PPP)**: "The most ancient universal glycolytic pathway"; intermediates used for nucleotide and amino acid biosynthesis.

#### Krebs Cycle Intermediates: Building Blocks
- Fatty acids and sterols
- Glutamate (and other amino acids)
- Porphyrins, heme, and chlorophyll
- Aspartate (and nucleotides)

Note: The Krebs cycle is both anabolic and catabolic.

### 8.3 Cellular Respiration

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
1. "Compare and contrast the electron transport system location and function in a prokaryotic cell and a eukaryotic cell"
2. "Compare and contrast the differences between substrate-level and oxidative phosphorylation"
3. "Explain the relationship between chemiosmosis and proton motive force"
4. "Describe the function and location of ATP synthase in a prokaryotic versus eukaryotic cell"
5. "Compare and contrast aerobic and anaerobic respiration"

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Electron Transport System (ETS)**: Series of membrane-associated protein complexes that transfer electrons from NADH and FADH₂ to final electron acceptors. Works like "a bucket brigade."

**Oxidative Phosphorylation**: ATP generation mechanism occurring when electron energy creates an electrochemical gradient across membranes. Produces the majority of cellular ATP.

**Proton Motive Force (PMF)**: "Electrochemical gradient formed by the accumulation of H⁺ on one side of the membrane compared with the other," establishing both electrical and chemical gradients.

**Chemiosmosis**: "Flow of hydrogen ions across the membrane" through ATP synthase down their electrochemical gradient — generates ATP.

**ATP Synthase**: "Complex protein that acts as a tiny generator, turning by the force of the H⁺ diffusing through the enzyme."
- Location in prokaryotes: plasma membrane
- Location in eukaryotes: inner mitochondrial membrane

**Aerobic Respiration**: Uses oxygen as final electron acceptor, producing water (H₂O).

**Anaerobic Respiration**: Uses inorganic molecules other than oxygen (nitrate, nitrite, sulfate) as final electron acceptors; generates smaller electrochemical gradients and less ATP.

#### ATP Yield Calculations (Aerobic Respiration)
- 1 NADH → ~3 ATP (via ETS)
- 1 FADH₂ → ~2 ATP (via ETS)
- Theoretical maximum from 1 glucose: **~38 ATP total**
  - 4 from substrate-level phosphorylation (glycolysis + Krebs)
  - ~34 from oxidative phosphorylation (ETS)
- Actual yield closer to 30–32 ATP due to membrane inefficiency.

#### Location Differences
- **Prokaryotes**: Pump H⁺ across cytoplasmic membrane to periplasmic space
- **Eukaryotes**: Pump H⁺ across inner mitochondrial membrane into intermembrane space

#### Clinical Relevance
The ETS components (cytochromes) are targets for some antimicrobial drugs. Differences between prokaryotic and eukaryotic ETS allow selective targeting.

### 9.1 How Microbes Grow

#### Learning Objectives

6. Identify health risks from biofilms and mitigation approaches
7. Describe quorum sensing and its role in cell-to-cell communication

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Binary Fission**: "The most common mechanism of cell replication in bacteria." DNA replication → chromosome segregation → cytokinesis → two identical daughter cells.

**Generation Time / Doubling Time**: "The time it takes for the population to double through one round of binary fission."
- *E. coli*: ~20 minutes (optimal)
- *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*: ~15-20 hours
- *M. leprae*: ~14 days

**FtsZ Protein**: "A protein called FtsZ assembles into a Z ring on the cytoplasmic membrane" to direct cell division and define the division plane. (Structurally analogous to tubulin in eukaryotes.)

**Growth Curve Phases**:
1. **Lag Phase**: "Cells are gearing up for the next phase of growth" — no population increase but intense metabolic activity. Cells adjusting to new environment.
2. **Log Phase (Exponential Growth)**: "Cells are actively dividing by binary fission and their number increases exponentially." Cells most susceptible to disinfectants and antibiotics during this phase.
3. **Stationary Phase**: "The number of new cells created by cell division is now equivalent to the number of cells dying." Secondary metabolites and virulence factors produced; endospore formation occurs.
4. **Death Phase**: "Exponential decrease in the number of cells" as nutrients deplete and toxins accumulate. "Persister cells" (slow metabolic rate) survive — important in chronic infections like tuberculosis.

**Colony-Forming Units (CFU)**: Units expressing viable plate counts.

**Biofilm**: "Complex and dynamic ecosystems that form on a variety of environmental surfaces" consisting of microorganisms embedded in extracellular matrix.

**Extracellular Polymeric Substances (EPS)**: "Hydrated gel composed primarily of polysaccharides" forming biofilm matrix; accounts for "50%–90% of the total dry mass."

**Quorum Sensing**: "Mechanism by which cells in a biofilm coordinate their activities" through release and binding of small, diffusible molecules called autoinducers. "When the cell population reaches a critical threshold (a quorum), these autoinducers initiate a cascade of reactions."

#### Population Growth Mathematics
Formula: Nn = N₀ × 2ⁿ
Where: Nn = cells at generation n; N₀ = initial cells; n = number of generations.
Example: Single cell dividing every 30 minutes for 24 hours ≈ 2.8 × 10¹⁴ cells.

#### Biofilm Formation Stages
1. **Reversible Attachment** (seconds): Planktonic cells attach to conditioned organic surface.
2. **Irreversible Attachment** (seconds–minutes): Cells express phenotypes facilitating EPS formation.
3. **Growth & Division** (hours–days): Cluster expansion on surface.
4. **EPS Production** (hours–days): Water channels form; appendages interact with matrix.
5. **Dispersal** (days–months): Peripheral cells revert to planktonic lifestyle.

#### Biofilms and Antibiotic Resistance
"Pathogens embedded within biofilms exhibit a higher resistance to antibiotics than their free-floating counterparts" due to:
- Metabolic inactivity in deep layers reducing antibiotic susceptibility
- EPS slowing antibiotic diffusion
- Increased efflux pump production actively extruding antibiotics
- Extrachromosomal DNA exchange facilitating resistance genes

#### Clinically Important Biofilm Examples
- Dental plaque → periodontal disease
- *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* in cystic fibrosis airways → chronic infections
- Wound infections
- Medical device colonization: catheters, artificial joints, contact lenses
- Airway biofilms cause aerobic/anaerobic layering — creating polymicrobial infections

### 9.2 Oxygen Requirements for Microbial Growth

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
1. "Interpret visual data demonstrating minimum, optimum, and maximum oxygen or carbon dioxide requirements for growth"
2. "Identify and describe different categories of microbes with requirements for growth with or without oxygen: obligate aerobe, obligate anaerobe, facultative anaerobe, aerotolerant anaerobe, microaerophile, and capnophile"
3. "Give examples of microorganisms for each category of growth requirements"

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Obligate (Strict) Aerobes**: Bacteria requiring abundant oxygen; cannot grow without it.
- Examples: *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, *Micrococcus luteus*, *Neisseria meningitidis*, *N. gonorrhoeae*

**Obligate Anaerobes**: Organisms killed by oxygen; thrive in oxygen-free environments.
- Examples: *Bacteroidetes*, *Clostridioides difficile*, *C. tetani*, *C. perfringens*

**Facultative Anaerobes**: Organisms thriving with oxygen but also surviving without it through fermentation or anaerobic respiration.
- Examples: Staphylococci, Enterobacteriaceae (including *E. coli*, *Salmonella*)

**Aerotolerant Anaerobes**: Indifferent to oxygen presence; possess fermentative metabolism but aren't harmed by oxygen.
- Examples: Lactobacilli, streptococci

**Microaerophiles**: Requiring minimal oxygen (1-10%), well below atmospheric 21%.
- Example: *Campylobacter jejuni*

**Capnophiles**: Preferring higher CO₂ and lower oxygen than atmospheric levels.

**Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS)**: "Highly unstable ions and molecules derived from partial reduction of oxygen that can damage virtually any macromolecule." Includes superoxide (O₂⁻), hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), hydroxyl radicals (OH•).

#### ROS Detoxification Enzymes
- **Superoxide Dismutase (SOD)**: Breaks down superoxide: 2O₂⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O₂ + O₂
- **Catalase**: Converts hydrogen peroxide: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂
- **Peroxidases**: Convert H₂O₂ to water using electron donors

Enzyme presence patterns:
- Obligate anaerobes: Lack all three enzymes
- Aerotolerant anaerobes: Possess SOD but lack catalase
- Facultative anaerobes: Possess catalase

**Catalase Test**: Mixing culture with 3% hydrogen peroxide; bubbling = catalase-positive. Distinguishes staphylococci (catalase-positive) from streptococci (catalase-negative).

#### Clinical Connection: Diabetic Foot Complications
Charles, a diabetic with a non-healing foot ulcer with purplish discoloration and oozing: suspected *Clostridium perfringens* infection (gas gangrene). Dead tissues lack blood circulation and oxygen → ideal obligate anaerobe environment. Approximately 70,000 US diabetic patients annually face foot/limb amputation.

Thioglycolate tube culture method: Used to determine oxygen requirements — bacterial growth pattern in gradient tube indicates organism category.

### 9.4 Temperature and Microbial Growth

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- "Illustrate and briefly describe minimum, optimum, and maximum temperature requirements for growth"
- "Identify and describe different categories of microbes with temperature requirements for growth: psychrophile, psychrotrophs, mesophile, thermophile, hyperthermophile"
- "Give examples of microorganisms in each category of temperature tolerance"

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Mesophiles** ("middle loving"): Optimal growth 20°C to ~45°C. The category that includes human pathogens (optimized for 37°C body temperature).
- Examples: *E. coli*, *Salmonella*, *Lactobacillus*

**Psychrotrophs** (psychrotolerant): Prefer cooler environments; function between 25°C and refrigeration temperatures (~4°C). Often responsible for spoiling refrigerated foods.

**Psychrophiles** (extreme cold-loving): Can survive at 0°C and below; optimal growth ~15°C. Cannot typically survive above 20°C.

**Thermophiles** ("heat loving"): Grow optimally between 50–80°C. Found in hot springs, geothermal soils, compost piles.

**Hyperthermophiles**: Growth ranges from 80–110°C. Examples: *Pyrobolus* and *Pyrodictium* archaea.

#### Clinical Connection: *Listeria monocytogenes*
- Gram-positive short rod; classified as psychrophilic and halotolerant.
- Multiplies at refrigeration temperatures (4–10°C) and tolerates high salt concentrations (up to 10% NaCl).
- "A frequent source of food poisoning" — 20% mortality rate.
- Particular risk to pregnant individuals: transplacental transmission.
- Treatment: High-dose ampicillin/penicillin G with aminoglycosides intravenously for 2 weeks.

#### Practical Application
*Taq* polymerase from *Thermus aquaticus* (thermophile) enables PCR technology — it survives the denaturation steps of the PCR cycle.

Each organism exhibits three critical temperature thresholds:
- Minimum growth temperature
- Optimum growth temperature (highest growth rates)
- Maximum growth temperature

Growth rate curves are "skewed toward the optimum temperature," reflecting rapid protein denaturation above optimal range.
