### 11.1 The Functions of Genetic Material

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- "Explain the two functions of the genome"
- "Explain the meaning of the central dogma of molecular biology"
- "Differentiate between genotype and phenotype and explain how environmental factors influence phenotype"

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Genome**: The complete collection of DNA within a cell, organized into discrete units called genes arranged on chromosomes and plasmids.

**Gene**: A segment of DNA that is transcribed to produce an RNA molecule.

**Messenger RNA (mRNA)**: A major RNA type providing information for ribosomal protein synthesis during translation.

**Transcription**: The process of reading DNA to produce RNA molecules.

**Translation**: The process catalyzed by ribosomes to synthesize proteins based on mRNA information.

**Gene Expression**: The synthesis of specific proteins with amino acid sequences encoded in genes through transcription and translation.

**Central Dogma of Molecular Biology**: "The flow of genetic information from DNA to RNA to protein." Three universal stages: initiation, elongation (polymerization), and termination.

**Genotype**: The complete collection of genes a cell contains.

**Phenotype**: "The set of observable characteristics that result from those genes," influenced by both genotype and environmental interactions.

#### Core Content

**Two Functions of DNA**:
1. **Inheritance Function**: DNA is passed from parent to offspring, requiring accurate replication with minimal errors.
2. **Protein Direction Function**: DNA directs and regulates construction of proteins necessary for cellular growth and reproduction.

"Cells carefully regulate expression of their genes, only using genes to make specific proteins when those proteins are needed." Environmental conditions influence which genes activate, producing different phenotypes from identical genotypes.

### 11.3 RNA Transcription

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- "Explain how RNA is synthesized using DNA as a template"
- "Distinguish between transcription in prokaryotes and eukaryotes"

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Transcription**: Process where "information encoded within the DNA sequence of one or more genes is transcribed into a strand of RNA."

**Template/Antisense Strand**: The DNA strand serving as the template for RNA synthesis.

**Sense Strand**: The nontemplate DNA strand whose sequence nearly matches the RNA product.

**Promoter**: "A DNA sequence onto which the transcription machinery binds and initiates transcription."

**Sigma Factor (σ)**: A subunit that "enables RNA polymerase to bind to a specific promoter."

**TATA Box**: The –10 consensus sequence (TATAAT) found in bacterial promoters.

**Exons**: "Coding sequences" expressed in the final protein (eukaryotes only).

**Introns**: "Intervening sequences" that are removed from pre-mRNA; don't encode functional polypeptides (eukaryotes only).

**RNA Splicing**: The "process of removing intron-encoded RNA sequences and reconnecting those encoded by exons."

**Alternative Splicing**: Process where different types of mRNA transcripts can be generated from the same DNA sequence — allows one gene to produce multiple protein variants.

**5' Cap**: A special 7-methylguanosine nucleotide added to protect eukaryotic mRNA from degradation.

**Poly-A Tail**: Approximately 200 adenine nucleotides added to 3' end of eukaryotic mRNA.

#### Bacterial Transcription Process
**Initiation**: Sigma factor recognizes and binds the –35 sequence; RNA polymerase initiates at promoter.

**Elongation**: σ subunit dissociates from the polymerase; RNA synthesis proceeds 5' to 3' at ~40 nucleotides per second. DNA unwinds ahead and rewinds behind the core enzyme.

**Termination**: DNA template includes repeated nucleotide sequences acting as termination signals; RNA polymerase stalls and releases the transcript.

Unlike DNA polymerase, "RNA polymerase does not require a primer to initiate synthesis."

#### Comparative Table: Prokaryotic vs. Eukaryotic Transcription
| Property | Bacteria | Eukaryotes |
|----------|----------|-----------|
| Polypeptides per mRNA | Mono/polycistronic | Exclusively monocistronic |
| Enzyme | One RNA polymerase | RNA Pol I, II, or III |
| 5' Cap Addition | No | Yes |
| 3' Poly-A Tail | No | Yes |
| Pre-mRNA Splicing (intron removal) | No | Yes |
| mRNA stability | ~5 seconds | Several hours |

#### Clinical Relevance
- Differences between prokaryotic and eukaryotic RNA polymerase allow selective targeting. **Rifampin** targets bacterial RNA polymerase specifically, without affecting human RNA polymerase.

### 11.5 Mutations

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- Compare point mutations and frameshift mutations
- Describe differences between missense, nonsense, and silent mutations
- Describe differences between light and dark repair mechanisms
- Explain how different mutagens act
- Explain why the Ames test detects carcinogens
- Analyze DNA sequences and identify mutation types

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Mutation**: "A heritable change in the DNA sequence of an organism."

**Wild Type**: "The phenotype most commonly observed in nature."

**Point Mutation**: Affects single base; most commonly involves base substitution.

**Silent Mutation**: Point mutation producing same amino acid despite sequence change; no protein effect. (Due to genetic code degeneracy)

**Missense Mutation**: Results in different amino acid incorporation; effects vary by chemical difference and location.

**Nonsense Mutation**: "Converts a codon encoding an amino acid into a stop codon" → premature termination of protein synthesis.

**Frameshift Mutation**: "Caused by insertions or deletions of a number of nucleotides that are not a multiple of three" — shifts reading frame, typically producing nonfunctional proteins.

**Insertion**: Addition of one or more bases to DNA sequence.

**Deletion**: Removal of one or more bases from DNA sequence.

**Mutagen**: "Various types of chemical agents or radiation" causing induced mutations; exposure increases mutation rate over 1000-fold.

**Carcinogen**: Agent that causes cancer.

**Nucleoside Analog**: "Structurally similar to normal nucleotide bases and can be incorporated into DNA during replication" → causes mutations.

**Intercalating Agent**: Molecules sliding between nitrogenous bases, distorting DNA and creating atypical spacing.

**Proofreading**: DNA polymerase function ensuring newly added bases complement template strand bases. Error rate: "one incorrect base per billion base pairs replicated."

**Nucleotide Excision Repair** (Dark Repair): "Enzymes remove the pyrimidine dimer and replace it with correct nucleotides."

**Direct Repair** (Light Repair / Photoreactivation): Uses visible light; "photolyase" enzyme recognizes thymine dimer distortion and breaks apart dimers. Absent in placental mammals.

**Auxotroph**: Nutritional mutant unable to synthesize specific nutrients due to biosynthesis pathway mutation.

**Ames Test**: "Method that uses bacteria for rapid, inexpensive screening of the carcinogenic potential of new chemical compounds." Uses histidine auxotroph *Salmonella typhimurium* strain; revertant colonies indicate mutagenicity.

#### Types of Mutagens
**Chemical Mutagens**:
- Nucleoside analogs (2-aminopurine, 5-bromouracil) — incorporated in place of normal bases
- Base-modifying agents (nitrous acid converting cytosine to uracil)
- Intercalating agents — cause DNA polymerase to skip or insert nucleotides

**Radiation**:
- Ionizing radiation (X-rays, gamma rays): Causes single/double-strand breaks and base modifications
- Nonionizing radiation (UV light): Induces thymine dimer formation — adjacent thymine bases link together, distorting DNA

#### Clinical Connection: CCR5-delta 32 Mutation
CCR5-delta 32 deletion mutation found in ~14% of Eurasian populations provides HIV resistance by preventing CCR5 coreceptor binding. This mutation may have been selected during plague or smallpox exposure. Demonstrates that not all mutations are harmful.

### 11.6 How Asexual Prokaryotes Achieve Genetic Diversity

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
1. "Compare the processes of transformation, transduction, and conjugation"
2. "Explain how asexual gene transfer results in prokaryotic genetic diversity"
3. "Explain the structure and consequences for bacterial genetic diversity of transposons"

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Vertical Gene Transfer**: Transmission of genetic material from generation to generation.

**Horizontal Gene Transfer (HGT)**: "The introduction of genetic material from one organism to another organism within the same generation," allowing even distantly related species to exchange genes. Critically important for spread of antibiotic resistance genes.

**Recombinant DNA**: "A molecule of DNA that contains fragments of DNA from different organisms."

**Competent Bacteria**: Cells that actively bind environmental DNA and can take it up.

#### Three Primary HGT Mechanisms

**1. Transformation**
- "Naked DNA is taken up from the environment"
- Cells lyse, releasing genomic material; competent bacteria take it up
- DNA may integrate into chromosome or remain as plasmid
- Clinical significance: acquisition of virulence factors and antibiotic resistance genes
- Example: *Streptococcus pneumoniae* acquired capsule genes via transformation

**2. Transduction**
- "Genes are transferred between cells in a virus" (bacteriophages)
- **Generalized Transduction**: Any chromosomal DNA accidentally packaged into phage head during assembly
- **Specialized Transduction**: Imprecise prophage excision carries adjacent bacterial chromosome segments
- **Lysogenic Conversion**: When lysogenic phage introduces virulence genes to new host
- Clinical Example: *Vibrio cholerae* pathogenicity results from CTX phage carrying cholera toxin gene integration. EHEC (*E. coli* O157:H7) acquired Shiga toxin gene from *Shigella* via phage transduction.

**3. Conjugation**
- "Use of a hollow tube called a conjugation pilus to transfer genes between cells"
- Requires direct cell-to-cell contact
- **F Plasmid (Fertility Factor)**: Encodes proteins for pilus formation and DNA transfer
  - **F+ cells (Donor)**: Contain F plasmid, form pilus
  - **F− cells (Recipient)**: Lack F plasmid, receive DNA; become F+
- **R Plasmids**: Encode antimicrobial resistance genes plus conjugation control genes; often contain MULTIPLE antibiotic resistance genes; transfer between same and different species → major driver of multidrug resistance

#### Transposition ("Jumping Genes")
**Transposons**: DNA sequences with inverted repeats and transposase gene encoding enzyme enabling movement. Found in prokaryotes and eukaryotes.
- **Nonreplicative**: "Cut-and-paste" movement (cut from original location, insert elsewhere)
- **Replicative**: "Copy and paste" (copy retained at original location, copy inserted elsewhere)
- Can inactivate or activate genes; relocate antibiotic resistance genes from chromosomes to plasmids

#### Clinical Significance for Nursing
- Antibiotic resistance genes spread rapidly through conjugation via R plasmids.
- A single patient carrying a resistant organism can seed an entire hospital unit via HGT.
- *Shigella* acquiring multiple resistance genes via transposons is why multidrug-resistant Shigella exists.
- Understanding HGT explains why completing antibiotic courses matters — subtherapeutic dosing selects for resistant mutants.

### 15.1 Characteristics of Infectious Disease

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
1. "Distinguish between signs and symptoms of disease"
2. "Explain the difference between a communicable disease and a noncommunicable disease"
3. "Compare different types of infectious diseases, including iatrogenic, nosocomial, and zoonotic diseases"
4. "Identify and describe the stages of an acute infectious disease in terms of number of pathogens present and severity of signs and symptoms"

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Disease**: "Any condition in which the normal structure or functions of the body are damaged or impaired."

**Signs**: "Objective and measurable, and can be directly observed by a clinician." Examples: fever temperature, blood pressure, WBC count.

**Symptoms**: "Subjective. Symptoms are felt or experienced by the patient, but they cannot be clinically confirmed or objectively measured." Examples: pain, nausea, fatigue.

**Syndrome**: "A specific group of signs and symptoms characteristic of a particular disease."

**Infectious Disease**: "Any disease caused by the direct effect of a pathogen."

**Communicable Disease**: "Capable of being spread from person to person through either direct or indirect mechanisms."

**Contagious Disease**: "Easily spread from person to person" (subset of communicable).

**Iatrogenic Diseases**: "Contracted as the result of a medical procedure." Examples: catheter-associated UTI, surgical site infection.

**Nosocomial Diseases**: "Acquired in hospital settings." Also called healthcare-associated infections (HAIs).

**Zoonotic Disease**: "A disease that occurs when a pathogen is transferred from a vertebrate animal to a human." Examples: rabies, Lyme disease, salmonellosis.

**Noncommunicable Infectious Disease**: "Not spread from one person to another." Examples: tetanus (*Clostridium tetani*), Legionnaires disease (*Legionella pneumophila*).

#### Normal Vital Signs
- Body temperature: 37°C (98.6°F)
- Heart rate: 60–100 beats per minute
- Breathing rate: 12–18 breaths per minute
- Blood pressure: 90/60 to 120/80 mm Hg

#### Five Stages/Periods of Acute Infectious Disease

1. **Incubation Period**: "Occurs in an acute disease after the initial entry of the pathogen into the host. It is during this time the pathogen begins multiplying in the host. However, there are insufficient numbers of pathogen particles present to cause signs and symptoms."

2. **Prodromal Period**: "The pathogen continues to multiply and the host begins to experience general signs and symptoms of illness." (Non-specific — often feels like a mild flu)

3. **Period of Illness**: "The signs and symptoms of disease are most obvious and severe." Highest pathogen load.

4. **Period of Decline**: "The number of pathogen particles begins to decrease, and the signs and symptoms of illness begin to decline." Immune response prevailing.

5. **Period of Convalescence**: "The patient generally returns to normal functions, although some diseases may inflict permanent damage." Patient may still be contagious during this period.

#### Disease Classification
**Acute Disease**: "Pathologic changes occur over a relatively short time (e.g., hours, days, or a few weeks) and involve a rapid onset."

**Chronic Disease**: "Pathologic changes can occur over longer time spans (e.g., months, years, or a lifetime)."

**Latent Disease**: "The causal pathogen goes dormant for extended periods of time with no active replication." Example: HSV, varicella-zoster, *M. tuberculosis*.

**Asymptomatic/Subclinical Infection**: No observable manifestations; patient may still be infectious.

#### Examples of Pathogens by Disease Type
**Common diarrheal disease agents**: *Vibrio cholerae*, *Listeria monocytogenes*, *Campylobacter jejuni*, enteropathogenic *E. coli*, norovirus, rotavirus, *Giardia lamblia*, *Cryptosporidium parvum*

**Zoonotic examples**: Rabies (viral), yellow fever (mosquito-transmitted), Rocky Mountain spotted fever (tick-transmitted *Rickettsia rickettsii*)

### 15.2 How Pathogens Cause Disease

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
1. "Summarize Koch's postulates and molecular Koch's postulates, respectively, and explain their significance and limitations"
2. "Explain the concept of pathogenicity (virulence) in terms of infectious and lethal dose"
3. "Distinguish between primary and opportunistic pathogens and identify specific examples of each"
4. "Summarize the stages of pathogenesis"
5. "Explain the roles of portals of entry and exit in the transmission of disease and identify specific examples of these portals"

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Koch's Postulates**: A systematic method established by Robert Koch for confirming causative relationships between diseases and specific pathogens.

**The Four Original Postulates**:
1. "The suspected pathogen must be found in every case of disease and not be found in healthy individuals."
2. "The suspected pathogen can be isolated and grown in pure culture."
3. "A healthy test subject infected with the suspected pathogen must develop the same signs and symptoms of disease as seen in postulate 1."
4. "The pathogen must be re-isolated from the new host and must be identical to the pathogen from postulate 2."

**Limitations of Koch's Postulates**:
- Pathogens exist in healthy individuals without causing disease (e.g., *H. pylori* in ~50% of population)
- Host susceptibility varies based on microbiota and immune status
- Not all pathogens can be cultured in pure form (obligate intracellular pathogens like *Rickettsia*, *Chlamydia*)
- Not all human diseases can be replicated in animal models (HIV only infects humans)

**Molecular Koch's Postulates** (Stanley Falkow, 1988):
1. "The phenotype (sign or symptom of disease) should be associated only with pathogenic strains of a species."
2. "Inactivation of the suspected gene(s) associated with pathogenicity should result in a measurable loss of pathogenicity."
3. "Reversion of the inactive gene should restore the disease phenotype."

**Pathogenicity**: "The ability of a microbial agent to cause disease."

**Virulence**: "The degree to which an organism is pathogenic" — exists on a continuum from avirulent to highly virulent.

**Infectious Dose (ID50)**: "The number of pathogen cells or virions required to cause active infection in 50% of inoculated animals."

**Lethal Dose (LD50)**: "The number of pathogenic cells, virions, or amount of toxin required to kill 50% of infected animals."

Note: Infective dose does NOT necessarily correlate with disease severity.
- *Salmonella* Typhimurium: ID50 ≥1 cell → salmonellosis (mild, <1% mortality)
- *S.* Typhi: ID50 ~1,000 cells → typhoid fever (severe, 10% mortality if untreated)

**Primary Pathogen**: "Can cause disease in a host regardless of the host's resident microbiota or immune system." Example: EHEC with Shiga toxin.

**Opportunistic Pathogen**: "Can only cause disease in situations that compromise the host's defenses." Examples:
- *Staphylococcus epidermidis*: Normal skin microbiota → pathogenic when introduced via catheters/implants
- *E. coli*: Normal in large intestine → urinary tract infections (leading female UTI cause)
- *Candida*: Opportunistic after antibiotics kill protective bacteria

**Adhesins**: "Molecules (either proteins or carbohydrates) called adhesins are found on the surface of certain pathogens and bind to specific receptors (glycoproteins) on host cells."

**Portal of Entry**: "An anatomic site through which pathogens can pass into host tissue."

**Portal of Exit**: Route for pathogen transmission from host to new host.

#### TORCH Infections (Cross Placental Barrier)
- **T**oxoplasmosis (*Toxoplasma gondii*)
- **O** — Syphilis (*Treponema pallidum*), Chickenpox, Hepatitis B, HIV, Fifth disease (Parvovirus B19)
- **R**ubella (Togavirus)
- **C**ytomegalovirus (Human herpesvirus 5)
- **H**erpes (HSV 1 and 2)

#### Stages of Pathogenesis

**Stage 1: Exposure (Contact)**: "An encounter with a potential pathogen." Major portals of entry:
- **Mucous membranes** (most important): Respiratory, gastrointestinal, genitourinary tracts
- **Skin**: Intact barrier or through breaches (wounds, bites, needle pricks)
- **Parenteral route**: Through breaks in protective barriers
- **Placenta**: Few pathogens cross blood-placental barrier (TORCH)

**Stage 2: Adhesion (Colonization)**: Pathogen attachment to host cells at portal of entry via adhesins.
- Bacteria use fimbriae, flagella, capsules, slime layers
- Viruses use capsids, spike proteins

**Stage 3: Invasion**: Dissemination of pathogen throughout local tissues or the body.
- Intracellular Pathogen Entry:
  - **Obligate intracellular pathogens** (reproduce ONLY inside cells): *Rickettsia*, *Chlamydia*
  - **Facultative intracellular pathogens** (inside OR outside): *Listeria*, *Shigella*
- Endocytosis mechanisms:
  - Effector protein-triggered membrane ruffling (*Salmonella*, *Shigella*)
  - Surface protein binding to host receptors (*Yersinia*)
- Phagocytosis evasion strategies:
  - Lyse phagosome before lysosome fusion (*Listeria*, *Shigella*)
  - Prevent phagosome-lysosome fusion (*M. tuberculosis*, *Legionella*, *Salmonella*)

**Stage 4: Infection**: Successful pathogen multiplication following invasion.
- **Local infection**: Confined to small area (e.g., *S. aureus* boil)
- **Focal infection**: Localized + toxins/bacteria spread to secondary location (e.g., dental → bloodstream)
- **Systemic infection**: Disseminated throughout body (e.g., varicella)

**Secondary Infections**: Initial infection compromises defenses; opportunistic pathogens exploit.
- Primary HIV → secondary oral thrush
- Primary influenza → secondary bacterial pneumonia (*H. influenzae*, *S. pneumoniae*)
- Antibiotic therapy → kills normal microbiota → *Candida* overgrowth

### 15.3 Virulence Factors of Bacterial and Viral Pathogens

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
1. Explain how virulence factors contribute to signs and symptoms of infectious disease
2. Differentiate between endotoxins and exotoxins
3. Describe and differentiate between various types of exotoxins
4. Describe the mechanisms viruses use for adhesion and antigenic variation

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Virulence Factors**: Unique molecules produced by individual pathogens that determine the extent and severity of disease.

**Adhesin**: Protein or glycoprotein on pathogen surface that attaches to receptors on host cells.

**Exoenzymes**: Extracellular enzymes produced by pathogens that enable invasion of host cells and deeper tissues.

**Toxigenicity**: The ability of a pathogen to produce toxins and cause damage to host cells.

**Endotoxin**: The lipopolysaccharide (LPS) found on the outer membrane of gram-negative bacteria.
- Source: Gram-negative bacteria outer membrane
- Heat stable (requires 121°C for 45 min to inactivate)
- LD50: High (0.24 mg/kg)
- Effect: General systemic inflammation and fever; excessive concentrations cause dangerous blood pressure drops
- Detection: Limulus amebocyte lysate (LAL) test

**Exotoxins**: Protein molecules produced by various pathogenic bacteria (mostly gram-positive, but some gram-negative).
- Source: Mostly gram-positive bacteria; secreted
- Heat labile (most denatured above 41°C)
- LD50: Very low (botulinum toxin: 0.000001 mg/kg — 240,000× more lethal than endotoxin)
- Effect: Specific damage to cells via receptor-mediated targeting

**Superantigens**: Exotoxins that trigger excessive, nonspecific stimulation of immune cells to secrete cytokines → **cytokine storm**.

**Cytokine Storm**: Excessive production of cytokines resulting in strong immune and inflammatory response → can cause fatal multi-organ failure.

**Antigenic Variation**: The alteration of surface proteins so a pathogen is no longer recognized by the host's immune system.

**Tropism**: Preferential targeting of viruses for specific cells, tissues, and organs.

**Antigenic Drift**: Slight changes in spike proteins caused by point mutations (influenza).

**Antigenic Shift**: Major changes in spike proteins due to gene reassortment in influenza viruses — can cause pandemics.

#### Endotoxin vs. Exotoxin Comparison
| Feature | Endotoxin | Exotoxin |
|---------|-----------|---------|
| Source | Gram-negative bacteria outer membrane | Mostly gram-positive bacteria |
| Chemical nature | Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) | Protein |
| Heat stability | Heat stable | Heat labile (mostly) |
| Toxicity (LD50) | High (less toxic per gram) | Very low (highly toxic) |
| Effect | General inflammation, fever | Specific cell damage |
| Example | LPS from *E. coli*, *N. meningitidis* | Botulinum toxin, cholera toxin |

#### Common Bacterial Adhesins
- *Streptococcus pyogenes*: Protein F → respiratory epithelial cells
- *Streptococcus mutans*: Adhesin P1 → teeth
- *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*: Type IV pili → urethral epithelial cells
- ETEC: Type 1 fimbriae → intestinal epithelial cells
- *Vibrio cholerae*: N-methylphenylalanine pili → intestinal epithelial cells

#### Exoenzymes and Their Clinical Effects
1. **Hyaluronidase** (*S. pyogenes*): Degrades hyaluronic acid cementing cells together → promotes tissue spreading; "spreading factor"
2. **DNAse** (*S. aureus*): Degrades extracellular DNA → prevents bacterial entrapment in NETs
3. **Phospholipase C** (*B. anthracis*): Degrades phospholipid bilayers → allows bacterial escape from phagosomes
4. **Collagenase** (*C. perfringens*): Digests collagen in connective tissue → promotes spread; can lead to gas gangrene

#### Septic Shock Mechanism
"Gram-negative bacteria are engulfed by phagocytes, which release tumor necrosis factor, increasing capillary permeability, causing edema and potentially fatal drop in blood pressure." This is why gram-negative sepsis is particularly dangerous.

Bacteremia terminology:
- **Bacteremia**: Bacteria in blood
- **Pyemia**: Pus-forming bacteria in blood
- **Viremia**: Viruses in blood
- **Toxemia**: Toxins in blood
- **Septicemia**: Bacteria present and multiplying in blood

#### Three Classes of Exotoxins

**Class 1: A-B Toxins (Intracellular-Targeting)**
Mechanism: B component binds to specific cell surface receptors → toxin enters via endocytosis → A component separates in acidic vacuole and enters cytoplasm where it acts.

Key examples:
- **Diphtheria Toxin** (*Corynebacterium diphtheriae*): A subunit transfers ADP-ribose to elongation factor 2 → inhibits protein synthesis → kills the cell
- **Cholera Toxin** (*Vibrio cholerae*): A subunit activates adenylyl cyclase → increases cAMP → intestinal epithelial cells secrete massive fluids and electrolytes → "rice-water stool" diarrhea
- **Botulinum Toxin** (*Clostridium botulinum*): A subunit cleaves proteins involved in acetylcholine release from neurons → blocks muscle contractions → **flaccid paralysis**. Most potent biological toxin known. Therapeutic applications: wrinkle removal, overactive bladder.
- **Tetanus Toxin** (*Clostridium tetani*): A subunit inhibits release of glycine and GABA inhibitory neurotransmitters → permanent muscle contraction → **spastic paralysis**. First symptom: lockjaw (trismus); culminates in respiratory failure.

Botulinum vs. Tetanus comparison:
- Botulinum: Blocks ACh release → **flaccid** paralysis (descending)
- Tetanus: Blocks inhibitory neurotransmitters → **spastic** paralysis (ascending)

**Class 2: Membrane-Disrupting Toxins**
Form pores or disrupt phospholipid bilayers:
- **Hemolysins** (e.g., Streptolysins O and S from *S. pyogenes*): Bind cholesterol in host cell membranes → form pores → cell lysis
- **Alpha toxin** (*S. aureus*), pneumolysin (*S. pneumoniae*)
- **Phospholipases**: Degrade phospholipid bilayers
- **Panton-Valentine Leukocidin (PVL)** (*S. aureus*): Associated with skin infections and necrotizing pneumonia; kills leukocytes

**Class 3: Superantigens**
- **Toxic Shock Syndrome Toxin-1 (TSST-1)** (*S. aureus*): Most common in menstruating females with vaginal colonization; bypass normal antigen processing → activate massive numbers of T cells → cytokine storm → life-threatening high fevers, low blood pressure, multi-organ failure, shock, death
- **Streptococcal Mitogenic Exotoxins** (*S. pyogenes*): Scarlet fever, streptococcal toxic shock syndrome

#### Virulence Factors for Immune Evasion
- **Capsules**: Prevent immune cell adhesion and phagocytosis; too large to engulf. *S. pneumoniae* encapsulated strains are more virulent.
- **Proteases**: Digest antibody molecules
- **M Protein** (*Streptococcus*): Inhibits phagocytosis by blocking complement molecule binding
- **Mycolic Acid** (*M. tuberculosis*): Waxy substance enables resistance to killing within phagolysosomes
- **Coagulase** (*S. aureus*): Exploits natural blood clotting → fibrin coating protects bacteria from phagocytic immune cells
- **Kinases** (staphylokinases, streptokinases): Trigger plasminogen → plasmin → digests fibrin clots; allow trapped bacteria to escape
- **Antigenic Variation**: *Borrelia burgdorferi* (Lyme disease) undergoes antigenic variation allowing chronic disease; *N. gonorrhoeae* varies type IV pili

#### Viral Virulence
**Viral Adhesins** define tropism:
- **Hemagglutinin** (Influenzavirus): Binds sialic acid on respiratory and intestinal cell membranes
- **Glycoprotein gp120** (HIV): Binds CD4 cellular marker and chemokine receptors (CCR5 or CXCR4) on immune system cells

**Antigenic Variation in Influenza**:
- **Antigenic Drift**: "Point mutations causing slight changes in hemagglutinin and neuraminidase spike proteins" over time → annual vaccine updates needed
- **Antigenic Shift**: "Major change in spike proteins due to gene reassortment occurring when two different influenza viruses infect the same host" → pandemic potential
