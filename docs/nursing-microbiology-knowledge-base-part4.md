### 14.1 History of Chemotherapy and Antimicrobial Discovery

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- "Compare and contrast natural, semisynthetic, and synthetic antimicrobial drugs"
- "Describe the chemotherapeutic approaches of ancient societies"
- "Describe the historically important individuals and events that led to the development of antimicrobial drugs"

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Chemotherapy**: "Any use of chemicals or drugs to treat disease," targeting either cancerous cells or infectious microorganisms.

**Antimicrobial Drugs**: Agents that "work by destroying or interfering with microbial structures and enzymes, either killing microbial cells or inhibiting their growth."

**Natural Antibiotic**: A compound produced naturally by organisms (e.g., penicillin from *Penicillium notatum*).

**Semisynthetic Antimicrobial**: "A chemically modified derivative of a natural antibiotic," designed to enhance bacterial targeting, stability, or reduce toxicity.

**Synthetic Antimicrobial**: "A drug that is developed from a chemical not found in nature" (e.g., sulfa drugs, fluoroquinolones).

#### Key Historical Figures
- **Paul Ehrlich** (1854-1915): Pioneered systematic drug screening; discovered Compound 606 (Salvarsan) targeting *Treponema pallidum* (syphilis) — first modern antimicrobial.
- **Gerhard Domagk**: Developed prontosil (sulfa drug); 1939 Nobel Prize.
- **Alexander Fleming** (1881-1955): Discovered penicillin accidentally in 1928 — mold contamination inhibited staphylococcal growth.
- **Howard Florey & Ernst Chain**: Purified penicillin and demonstrated clinical efficacy in 1940; 1945 Nobel Prize.
- **Dorothy Hodgkin** (1910-1994): Used X-ray crystallography to determine penicillin's structure in 1946; enabled semisynthetic penicillin development; 1964 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
- **Selman Waksman** (1888-1973): Led discovery of actinomycin, streptomycin, and neomycin from *Streptomyces* bacteria. "The actinomycetes are the source of more than half of all natural antibiotics." 1952 Nobel Prize.

#### Ancient Antimicrobial Use
- Nubian skeletal remains (350–550 CE): Tetracycline found at high concentrations from purposeful fermentation in beer.
- Chinese researcher Tu Youyou: Won Nobel Prize 2015 for identifying *Artemisia* (4th-century text) treating malaria.
- Healers used moldy bread to treat wounds for centuries (pre-Fleming).

### 14.3 Mechanisms of Antibacterial Drugs

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
"Describe the mechanisms of action associated with drugs that inhibit cell wall biosynthesis, protein synthesis, membrane function, nucleic acid synthesis, and metabolic pathways"

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Selective Toxicity**: Capacity to eliminate or suppress microorganisms while causing negligible harm to host organisms.

**Bactericidal**: Drugs that actively KILL bacterial cells.

**Bacteriostatic**: Drugs that INHIBIT bacterial growth without directly killing cells (immune system must clear remaining organisms).

**β-lactam Ring**: Core structural component shared across penicillins, cephalosporins, monobactams, and carbapenems — inhibits cell wall synthesis.

**Penicillin-Binding Proteins (PBPs)**: Transpeptidase enzymes targeted by β-lactam antibiotics — required for peptidoglycan crosslinking.

#### Major Antibacterial Drug Classes by Mechanism

**1. Cell Wall Biosynthesis Inhibitors**
(Exploiting the fact that human cells have no peptidoglycan)
- **β-lactams** (penicillins, cephalosporins, monobactams, carbapenems): Block peptidoglycan crosslinking by binding PBPs → weakened cell wall → osmotic lysis
- **Glycopeptides** (vancomycin): Bind peptide chains; prevent incorporation into peptidoglycan backbone; effective against gram-positive bacteria
- **Bacitracin**: Blocks transport of peptidoglycan precursors across cytoplasmic membrane; topical use only (nephrotoxic systemically)

β-lactam generations (cephalosporins):
- 1st generation (cephalexin): Primarily gram-positive
- 2nd generation (cefuroxime): Extended gram-negative
- 3rd generation (ceftriaxone): Broad gram-negative; crosses BBB
- 4th generation (cefepime): Anti-pseudomonal
- 5th generation (ceftaroline): Active against MRSA

**2. Protein Synthesis Inhibitors**
(Exploiting the 70S vs. 80S ribosome difference)

Drugs targeting the **30S subunit**:
- **Aminoglycosides** (gentamicin, tobramycin, amikacin): Impair proofreading → faulty protein production → cell death. Bactericidal. Side effects: **nephrotoxic, neurotoxic, ototoxic** (hearing loss).
- **Tetracyclines** (tetracycline, doxycycline, minocycline): Block tRNA association with ribosomes → bacteriostatic. Side effects: phototoxic, dental discoloration in children, avoid in pregnancy. Broad spectrum.

Drugs targeting the **50S subunit**:
- **Macrolides** (erythromycin, azithromycin, clarithromycin): Inhibit peptide bond formation → bacteriostatic. Used for atypical pneumonia, community-acquired pneumonia, STIs. Azithromycin = "Z-pack" (5-day course).
- **Lincosamides** (clindamycin): Similar action to macrolides; excellent tissue penetration; effective against anaerobes. Risk of *C. difficile* colitis.
- **Chloramphenicol**: Prevents peptide bond formation; causes reversible and irreversible aplastic anemia; **gray baby syndrome** in neonates. Reserved for life-threatening infections.
- **Oxazolidinones** (linezolid): Interfere with initiation complex formation; MRSA and VRE coverage.

**3. Membrane Function Disruptors**
- **Polymyxins** (colistin, polymyxin B): Target lipopolysaccharide in gram-negative outer membranes; disrupt outer membrane → leakage → death. Last resort for MDR gram-negatives. Nephrotoxic.
- **Daptomycin**: Targets gram-positive bacterial membranes specifically; effective against MRSA and VRE. Cannot use for pneumonia (inactivated by lung surfactant).

**4. Nucleic Acid Synthesis Inhibitors**
- **Rifamycins** (rifampin, rifaximin): Block bacterial RNA polymerase activity — does NOT inhibit human RNA polymerase (different structure). Used for TB, meningococcal prophylaxis. Turns urine/sweat/tears orange-red.
- **Fluoroquinolones** (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin): Inhibit DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II) activity → prevent DNA supercoiling unwinding → bactericidal. Broad spectrum. Side effects: phototoxic, neurotoxic, cardiotoxic (QT prolongation), **tendon rupture** risk (especially Achilles in patients >60, on corticosteroids, or renal failure).

**5. Metabolic Pathway Antimetabolites**
(Exploiting the fact that humans get folate from food; bacteria must synthesize it)
- **Sulfonamides** (sulfamethoxazole): Structural analogs of PABA; block dihydrofolic acid synthesis → bacteriostatic.
- **Trimethoprim**: Blocks tetrahydrofolic acid synthesis (next step); synergistic with sulfonamides.
- **TMP-SMX (Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole, Bactrim)**: Double blockade of folate pathway → bactericidal. Used for UTIs, PCP (Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia), MRSA skin infections.
- **Isoniazid (INH)**: Prodrug preventing mycolic acid synthesis in mycobacteria → specific for TB/mycobacteria. Hepatotoxic; give with pyridoxine (B6) to prevent peripheral neuropathy.

**6. ATP Synthase Inhibitors**
- **Diarylquinolines** (bedaquiline): Interfere with mycobacterial ATP production; used for MDR-TB.

#### Drug Side Effect Summary for Nursing
| Drug Class | Key Side Effects |
|-----------|----------------|
| Aminoglycosides | Nephrotoxic, ototoxic (hearing loss), neurotoxic |
| Tetracyclines | Phototoxic, dental discoloration (children), avoid pregnancy |
| Chloramphenicol | Aplastic anemia, gray baby syndrome |
| Fluoroquinolones | Tendon rupture, phototoxic, QT prolongation, neurotoxic |
| Clindamycin | *C. difficile* colitis risk |
| Rifampin | Orange-red body fluids; many drug interactions (CYP450 inducer) |
| Isoniazid | Hepatotoxic, peripheral neuropathy (prevent with B6) |
| Vancomycin | Nephrotoxic; Red Man Syndrome (if infused too fast) |
| Polymyxins | Nephrotoxic, neurotoxic |

### 14.5 Drug Resistance

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- "Explain the concept of drug resistance"
- "Describe how microorganisms develop or acquire drug resistance"
- "Describe the different mechanisms of antimicrobial drug resistance"

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Antimicrobial Resistance**: Evolution of microbes to overcome antimicrobial compounds through natural processes and selective pressures.

**Horizontal Gene Transfer**: Movement of resistance genes between microbes via plasmids and transposons.

**Multidrug-Resistant (MDR) Microbes**: "Colloquially known as 'superbugs' and carry one or more resistance mechanism(s)."

**Cross-Resistance**: Single resistance mechanism conferring resistance to multiple antimicrobial drugs simultaneously.

**ESKAPE Pathogens** (the most clinically problematic MDR organisms):
- **E** — *Enterococcus faecium*
- **S** — *Staphylococcus aureus*
- **K** — *Klebsiella pneumoniae*
- **A** — *Acinetobacter baumannii*
- **P** — *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*
- **E** — *Enterobacter* spp.

Statistics: Superbugs responsible for "more than 2 million infections in the US annually" and "at least 23,000 fatalities."

#### Five Major Resistance Mechanisms

**1. Drug Modification/Inactivation**
Enzymes chemically modify or destroy antimicrobials:
- **β-lactamases**: "Enzymatic hydrolysis of the β-lactam bond" → disables all β-lactam drugs
- **Extended-Spectrum β-Lactamases (ESBLs)**: Resist penicillins, cephalosporins, monobactams
- **Carbapenemases**: Destroy even carbapenems (last-resort drugs)
- **New Delhi Metallo-β-lactamase (NDM)**: Increasingly common in travelers from India, surrounding regions
- Aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes

**2. Prevention of Cellular Uptake / Efflux**
- Outer membrane lipid composition changes
- Porin channel alterations (reduce drug entry)
- **Efflux pumps**: "Actively transport an antimicrobial drug out of the cell" before it can act
- *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* decreases OprD porin, blocking carbapenem entry

**3. Target Modification**
"Structural changes to those targets can prevent drug binding, rendering the drug ineffective."
- Altered Penicillin-Binding Proteins (PBPs) → MRSA resistance to all β-lactams
- Ribosome modifications (aminoglycoside, macrolide resistance)
- RNA polymerase mutations (rifampin resistance)
- DNA gyrase mutations (fluoroquinolone resistance)
- Altered peptidoglycan precursors (vancomycin resistance)

**4. Target Overproduction / Enzymatic Bypass**
- Overproducing target enzymes to maintain function despite inhibition
- Developing metabolic bypasses
- Vancomycin resistance: Decreased peptide chain cross-linkage traps drug in outer cell wall

**5. Target Mimicry**
Recently discovered mechanism: Bacteria produce proteins mimicking drug targets, preventing binding.
- MfpA (Mycobacterium fluoroquinolone resistance protein A): Resembles DNA → DNA gyrase binds to protein instead of fluoroquinolone

#### Clinically Critical Resistant Organisms

**Methicillin-Resistant *Staphylococcus aureus* (MRSA)**:
- Acquired low-affinity PBP (PBP2a) conferring resistance to ALL β-lactams
- HA-MRSA (hospital-associated): IV lines, catheters, immunocompromised
- CA-MRSA (community-associated): Skin/soft tissue infections; sports teams, prisons
- About 6% of *S. aureus* nasal carriers are MRSA; >90% of *S. aureus* clinical isolates penicillin-resistant
- Treatment: Vancomycin (IV), TMP-SMX or doxycycline (oral for skin infections), linezolid, daptomycin

**Vancomycin-Resistant Enterococci (VRE)**:
- Target modification changing peptidoglycan structure → vancomycin cannot bind

**Vancomycin-Intermediate *S. aureus* (VISA)** and **Vancomycin-Resistant *S. aureus* (VRSA)**:
- MIC 4–8 µg/mL (VISA); MIC ≥16 µg/mL (VRSA)
- VRSA acquired resistance horizontally from VRE

**Multidrug-Resistant *Mycobacterium tuberculosis***:
- **MDR-TB**: Resistant to rifampin AND isoniazid
- **XDR-TB**: Additionally resistant to fluoroquinolones AND aminoglycosides — severely limited treatment options

**Carbapenem-Resistant Organisms (CRO)**: Pan-resistant strains emerging — resistant to ALL available antibacterials in some cases.

#### Contributing Factors to Resistance Development
- Overuse and misuse of antimicrobials (e.g., viral infections treated with antibiotics)
- Inappropriate antimicrobial selection
- Subtherapeutic dosing
- Patient noncompliance (not completing courses)
- Agricultural use: CAFOs use antimicrobials extensively for growth promotion → select resistant bacteria that can transfer genes to human pathogens

#### Clavulanic Acid (β-lactamase Inhibitor)
"Clavulanic acid is a molecule produced by *Streptomyces clavuligerus*" — binds irreversibly within the active site of β-lactamases, preventing them from inactivating a coadministered penicillin.
- **Augmentin** = amoxicillin + clavulanic acid (oral)
- **Unasyn** = ampicillin + sulbactam (IV)
- **Zosyn** = piperacillin + tazobactam (IV)

### 21.1 Anatomy and Normal Microbiota of the Skin and Eyes

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- Describe major anatomical features of the skin and eyes
- Compare microbiomes across body sites
- Explain how microorganisms overcome skin and eye defenses to cause infection
- Describe general signs and symptoms of skin and eye infection diseases

#### Skin Layers and Defense
- **Epidermis**: Outermost layer; stratum corneum contains dead cells rich in keratin — physical barrier
- **Dermis**: Blood vessels, nerves, hair follicles, sweat glands, sebaceous glands
- **Hypodermis**: Fibrous and adipose connective tissue beneath dermis

Skin defenses:
- Physical barrier via keratin protein
- Antimicrobial substances in sweat (salts, lysozyme, antimicrobial peptides)
- Sebum lipids inhibit growth
- Dry, salty, acidic environment (pH ~5) hostile to most pathogens
- Normal microbiota outcompetes pathogens

#### Normal Skin Microbiota by Region
- Dry areas: Betaproteobacteria predominate
- Sebum-rich areas: *Propionibacterium/Cutibacterium* predominate
- Moist areas: *Corynebacterium* and *Staphylococcus* predominate
- Fungi: *Malassezia* most common

#### Eye Anatomy and Defense
- **Lacrimal apparatus**: Tear production and drainage system
- **Conjunctiva**: Mucous membranes covering eyeball and inner eyelid
- Tear chemicals (defensins, lactoferrin, lysozyme) — first-line eye defense

#### Medical Terminology for Skin Lesions
| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| Abscess | Localized pus collection |
| Furuncle | Hair follicle infection causing pus-filled bump (boil) |
| Carbuncle | Group of interconnected furuncles |
| Pustule | Fluid- or pus-filled bump |
| Vesicle | Small fluid-filled lesion |
| Macule | Smooth skin discoloration |
| Papule | Small raised bump |
| Ulcer | Break in skin; open sore |
| Eschar | Area of dead (necrotic) tissue; black eschar in anthrax |

#### Eye Infection Terminology
- **Conjunctivitis** ("pinkeye"): Conjunctiva inflammation
- **Blepharitis**: Eyelid inflammation
- **Keratitis**: Cornea inflammation
- **Dacryocystitis**: Lacrimal sac inflammation

### 21.2 Bacterial Infections of the Skin and Eyes

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- "Identify the most common bacterial pathogens that cause infections of the skin and eyes"
- "Compare the major characteristics of specific bacterial diseases affecting the skin and eyes"

#### Staphylococcal Skin Infections

**Key Pathogen**: *Staphylococcus aureus* — gram-positive cocci forming grape-like clusters; commonly found in nasal passages; produces virulence factors including hemolysins, coagulase, leukocidins, and Protein A.

**MRSA** (Methicillin-Resistant *S. aureus*):
- Hospital-acquired (HA-MRSA): IV lines, catheters, surgical sites
- Community-acquired (CA-MRSA): Skin/soft tissue; contact sports, close quarters
- Initial empiric therapy: TMP-SMX, clindamycin, tetracyclines, or linezolid

**Identification Tests**:
- Catalase test: Catalase-positive (distinguishing Staphylococcus from catalase-negative Streptococcus)
- Coagulase test: *S. aureus* = coagulase-positive (CoPS); *S. epidermidis* = coagulase-negative (CoNS)
- Mannitol Salt Agar (MSA): *S. aureus* ferments mannitol → yellow colonies

**Folliculitis**: Superficial bumps/pimples; may be self-limiting; topical antibiotics if persistent.

**Furuncles (Boils) and Carbuncles**: Deeper infections; common in athletes, immunocompromised, close-quarters populations. May require drainage and antibiotics if spreading or systemic symptoms.

**Staphylococcal Scalded Skin Syndrome (SSSS)**:
- Exotoxin (epidermolytic toxin)-induced erythema and severe skin peeling
- Most common in young children/infants
- Treatment: intravenous antibiotics and fluid therapy

**Impetigo**:
- "Formation of vesicles, pustules, and possibly bullae, often around the nose and mouth"
- Nonbullous form: Ruptures into honey-colored encrusted sores
- Bullous form: Larger draining lesions (bullae ≥5 mm), *S. aureus* toxin-mediated
- Caused by *S. aureus* alone, *S. pyogenes* alone, or coinfection
- Highly contagious, especially in children
- Topical/oral antibiotics effective
- *S. pyogenes* impetigo risks acute glomerulonephritis (AGN) as a sequela

**Staphylococcus epidermidis Nosocomial Infections**:
- Harmless resident of normal skin microbiota
- Causes opportunistic infections when transferred via medical devices (catheters, prostheses, indwelling devices)
- Antibiotic-resistant biofilms; strict sterilization procedures required

#### Streptococcal Skin Infections

**Key Pathogen**: *Streptococcus pyogenes* (Group A Streptococcus) — gram-positive cocci in chains; catalase-negative.

**Characteristics**: Small (1–2 mm) translucent colonies; nonhemolytic, alpha-hemolytic, or beta-hemolytic on blood agar; facultative anaerobes.

**Cellulitis**: "Reddened area of the skin that is warm to the touch and painful" developing in dermis/hypodermis; often caused by *S. pyogenes* breaching skin via cuts/abrasions. Treatment: penicillin or amoxicillin.

**Erysipelas**: "Large, intensely inflamed patch of skin involving the dermis" (often legs/face); distinct raised border; may be bullous. Treatment: penicillin.

**Erythema Nodosum**: Inflammation in subcutaneous fat; "red nodules on the skin, most frequently on the shins"; not suppurative; often self-limiting; treated with NSAIDs, cool compresses, elevation, bed rest.

**Necrotizing Fasciitis** ("Flesh-Eating Bacterial Syndrome"):
- Infection of fascia (connective tissue between skin/muscle)
- "Rapidly spreading" tissue death
- Caused by *S. pyogenes*, *Klebsiella*, *Clostridium*, *E. coli*, *S. aureus*, or *Aeromonas hydrophila*
- Virulence factors: bacterial proteases destroy tissues, inactivate complement, prevent neutrophil migration
- Requires urgent surgical debridement/amputation plus intravenous antibiotics
- Mortality: ~30% even with treatment

#### *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* Skin Infections

**Key Characteristics**: Gram-negative, oxidase-positive, aerobic bacillus; produces blue-green pigments (pyocyanin, pyoverdin) and distinctive grape soda/corn tortilla odor from 2-aminoacetophenone.

**Hot Tub Folliculitis**: Folliculitis from pool/hot tub exposure; *P. aeruginosa* in warm water.

**Otitis Externa** ("Swimmer's Ear"): Itching, redness, discomfort of ear canal; progresses to fever, pain, swelling. Treatment: ear drops containing acetic acid, antibacterials, steroids.

**Wound Infections**: Blue-green pus from pigments; produces phospholipase C (hemolysin), exoenzyme S, exotoxin A, slime layer, fimbriae, proteases. Extremely antibiotic-resistant; effective agents: polymyxin B, gentamicin, fluoroquinolones.

#### Acne (*Cutibacterium acnes*)

**Pathogen**: *Cutibacterium acnes* (formerly *Propionibacterium acnes*) — gram-positive, non-spore-forming, aerotolerant anaerobic bacillus that consumes sebum.

**Mechanism**: Hair follicles clogged by shed skin cells and sebum → comedones; *C. acnes* consumes sebum, secretes enzymes damaging follicles → papules, pustules, nodules.
- Whiteheads: Covered by skin
- Blackheads: Exposed to air, oxidized lipids appear dark

**Treatment by Severity**:
- Mild: Topical salicylic acid, retinoids
- Moderate: Antibiotics (erythromycin, clindamycin), benzoyl peroxide, hormones
- Severe: Isotretinoin (strong retinoid; teratogenic — requires iPLEDGE program; photosensitivity risks), phototherapy, laser therapy

#### Anthrax (*Bacillus anthracis*)

**Agent**: *B. anthracis* — gram-positive, endospore-forming, facultative anaerobe; zoonotic disease.

**Cutaneous Anthrax** (95–99% of cases):
- Endospores enter through skin abrasions
- Produces painless ulcer → "black eschar" (mass of dead skin tissue)
- Can progress to bacteremia/septicemia
- 20% mortality if untreated; <1% with treatment
- Virulence: capsule (phagocytosis prevention) + binary exotoxins: **lethal toxin** (protective antigen + lethal factor) and **edema toxin** (protective antigen + edema factor)

**Other Forms**:
- Gastrointestinal: 40% mortality with treatment
- Inhalation/pulmonary: 45% treated mortality, 85% untreated
- Injection anthrax: Emerging in IV drug users; high mortality

**Treatment**: Penicillin, erythromycin, tetracycline.

#### Bacterial Conjunctivitis

**Acute Purulent Conjunctivitis**:
- "Inflammation of the conjunctiva, often accompanied by a discharge of sticky fluid"
- Most commonly *H. influenzae* but also *M. catarrhalis*, *S. pneumoniae*, *S. aureus*
- Very contagious via secretions; self-limiting but treatment recommended
- Contact lens users should discontinue wear

**Neonatal Conjunctivitis (Ophthalmia Neonatorum)**:
Two forms contracted through birth canal:
- **Gonococcal** (*N. gonorrhoeae*): Can cause corneal ulceration/perforation leading to blindness if untreated
- **Chlamydial / Inclusion Conjunctivitis** (*C. trachomatis*): Similar severe complications

Prevention: Erythromycin ointment applied to newborn eyes — legal requirement in most hospitals.
Treatment: Aggressive oral/intravenous antibiotics.

**Trachoma**:
- "Common cause of preventable blindness" — rare in US but widespread in Africa/Asia
- Caused by *C. trachomatis*; chronic conjunctivitis → necrotic follicles and scarring
- Transmission: fomites (contaminated towels, linens), direct contact, eye-seeking flies
- Complication: Trichiasis — "eyelashes turn inward" → corneal abrasion → blindness
- Treatment: Azithromycin effective when treated promptly
- WHO SAFE Initiative: Surgery, Antibiotics, Facial cleanliness, Environmental improvement

### 22.1 Anatomy and Normal Microbiota of the Respiratory Tract

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
1. Describe major anatomical features of upper and lower respiratory tract
2. Describe normal microbiota of upper and lower respiratory tracts
3. Explain how microorganisms overcome respiratory-tract membrane defenses to cause infection
4. Explain microbe-respiratory system interactions in healthy individuals and during infection

#### Upper Respiratory System Anatomy
- **Nares** (nostrils): External openings lined with hairs trapping particles
- **Nasal cavity**: Lined with mucous membrane; Bowman's glands produce mucus
- **Sinuses**: Four paired skull cavities communicating with nasal cavity
- **Nasopharynx**: Upper throat extending from posterior nasal cavity
- **Eustachian tube**: Connects middle ear to nasopharynx (important in otitis media)
- **Pharynx**: Nasopharynx, oropharynx, laryngopharynx
- **Palatine tonsils**: Lymphoid tissue in oropharynx
- **Larynx**: Contains vocal folds

#### Lower Respiratory System Anatomy
- **Trachea**: Cartilaginous tube providing unobstructed path for air
- **Bronchi/Bronchioles**: Branching tubes from trachea
- **Alveoli**: Terminal cul-de-sacs; site of gas exchange; ~400 million present; covered by thin epithelium with alveolar macrophages
- **Pleural membrane**: Double-layered protective structure

#### Respiratory Defenses
- **Mucociliary Escalator**: Beating cilia dislodge and propel mucus/trapped microbes upward
- Goblet cells secrete sticky mucus layer
- Nasal hairs trap large particles
- Mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT): adenoids and tonsils
- Secreted antibodies (IgA), lysozyme, surfactant, and antimicrobial peptides called defensins
- Alveolar macrophages in lower tract

"As much as 20% of the population carry *Staphylococcus aureus* in their nostrils" — potential reservoir.

#### Signs and Symptoms (by Anatomical Location)
- **Rhinitis**: Nasal cavity inflammation
- **Sinusitis**: Sinus inflammation
- **Otitis media**: Middle ear inflammation
- **Pharyngitis**: Sore throat
- **Laryngitis**: Larynx inflammation (may cause voice loss)
- **Tonsillitis**: Tonsil inflammation
- **Epiglottitis**: Epiglottis infection (airway emergency)
- **Bronchitis**: Bronchial tube inflammation
- **Pneumonia**: Alveoli infection; "pus and edema accumulate and fill the alveoli with fluids"

Tobacco smoke impairs defenses: disrupts ciliated epithelial cells, inhibits phagocytosis, blocks antimicrobial peptides → mucociliary escalator dysfunction → increased pneumonia risk.

### 22.2 Bacterial Infections of the Respiratory Tract

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- "Identify the most common bacteria that can cause infections of the upper and lower respiratory tract"
- "Compare the major characteristics of specific bacterial diseases of the respiratory tract"

#### Streptococcal Pharyngitis (Strep Throat)

**Pathogen**: *Streptococcus pyogenes* (Group A Streptococcus / GAS)

**Symptoms**: "Fever higher than 38°C (100.4°F); intense pharyngeal pain; erythema; swollen, dark-red palatine tonsils" with petechiae. Exudates possible. Notable ABSENCE of cough and runny nose (which suggest viral).

**Serious Sequelae of Untreated Strep**:
- **Acute Rheumatic Fever** (1-3% untreated cases): Molecular mimicry — M protein cross-reacts with heart tissue antigens → carditis, arthritis, chorea
- **Acute Glomerulonephritis**: Immune complex formation and deposition in glomeruli → kidney damage

**Scarlet Fever**: Caused by strains with phage-encoded erythrogenic toxin (superantigen); characteristic "sandpaper" rash + strawberry tongue; may progress to streptococcal toxic shock syndrome (STSS).

**Diagnosis**: Rapid enzyme immunoassay (up to 30% false-negatives); culture = gold standard; throat swab for culture.

**Treatment**: Amoxicillin or penicillin G. Antibiotic resistance remains limited for GAS.

#### Acute Otitis Media (AOM)

Most common pediatric infection (ages 3 months to 3 years). "Moderate to severe bulging of the tympanic membrane and otalgia (ear pain)."

**Common Causative Agents**:
- Neonates: *S. pneumoniae*, *E. coli*, *Enterococcus*, Group B *Streptococcus*
- Older children: *S. pneumoniae*, *H. influenzae*, *M. catarrhalis*

Risk factors: Shorter eustachian tubes in children, more upper respiratory infections, bottle-feeding while recumbent.

**Treatment**: High-dose amoxicillin first-line. PCV13 vaccination reduces incidence.

#### Diphtheria

**Pathogen**: *Corynebacterium diphtheriae*

**Toxin**: Diphtheria toxin — A-B toxin encoded by temperate bacteriophage; A subunit "blocks host-cell protein synthesis by inactivating elongation factor (EF)-2."

**Pathology**: Forms "pseudomembrane" (gray, leathery exudate of dead cells, pus, fibrin, bacteria) covering nasopharynx/tonsils/pharynx/larynx — can obstruct airway → suffocation.

**Systemic Effects**: Myocarditis and nerve damage possible.

**Treatment**: Penicillin/erythromycin control infection; antitoxins (horse serum) neutralize preformed toxin.

**Prevention**: DTaP, Tdap, DT, Td vaccines. Five childhood doses plus decennial boosters (Td) recommended.

#### Bacterial Pneumonia

General inflammation of alveoli with fluid/leukocyte accumulation impairing gas exchange. Over 50,000 deaths annually in US.

**Pneumococcal Pneumonia** (*Streptococcus pneumoniae*):
- Lancet-shaped diplococci; alpha-hemolytic on blood agar
- Polysaccharide capsule interferes with phagocytosis
- Productive cough with bloody (rust-colored) sputum; fever, pleuritic chest pain
- Identification: Gram stain morphology, optochin sensitivity, quellung reaction (capsule swelling)
- Treatment: β-lactams first-line; increasing resistance to macrolides/fluoroquinolones
- Vaccines: PCV13 (children + adults ≥65), PPSV23 (adults ≥65 + high-risk)

**Haemophilus Influenzae Pneumonia**:
- Small gram-negative coccobacillus; nonencapsulated strains cause pneumonia
- Primarily in elderly
- Requires factors X (hemin) and V (NAD); grows on chocolate agar
- Increasing β-lactam, macrolide, tetracycline resistance

**Mycoplasma Pneumonia ("Walking Pneumonia")** (*Mycoplasma pneumoniae*):
- Lacks cell wall → **β-lactams ineffective**
- Uses specialized attachment organelle; damages ciliated epithelial cells
- Mild disease with low fever and persistent cough — patient can walk around
- Common in crowded environments (colleges, military barracks)
- Self-limiting; responds to macrolides (azithromycin, clarithromycin) and tetracyclines

**Chlamydial Pneumonias**:
- *C. pneumoniae*, *C. psittaci*, *C. trachomatis*
- Obligate intracellular pathogens → cannot culture on standard media
- *C. psittaci* causes psittacosis (zoonotic from birds)
- Diagnosis: PCR or serology
- Treatment: Tetracycline or macrolides

**Health Care-Associated Pneumonia (HCAP)**:
- *Klebsiella pneumoniae*: "Currant jelly sputum" from thick capsule; lung necrosis; often fatal despite treatment; multidrug resistance common
- *S. aureus*, *E. coli*, *Proteus*, *Serratia*
- Risk factors: Intubation, antibiotics, immunomodulatory drugs, medical devices

**Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia (VAP)**:
- *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*: Chronic lung infection in cystic fibrosis; produces alginate exopolysaccharide; chronically colonizes CF airways; leading cause of CF mortality. Extremely antibiotic-resistant.

#### Tuberculosis (TB)

**Pathogen**: *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* — acid-fast, high G+C content, gram-positive rod with waxy **mycolic acids** in cell wall making cells impervious to polar molecules; slow growth (20-minute generation time is fast compared to its 15-20 hour doubling time).

**Transmission**: Inhalation of respiratory droplets; infectious dose only ~10 cells.

**Pathophysiology**:
1. Bacteria phagocytized by alveolar macrophages but survive within due to mycolic acid protection (prevents phagolysosome fusion)
2. Weeks/months elapse before T/B cell immune response
3. Lesions wall off forming **tubercles** with caseous (cheese-like necrotic) centers → **primary TB** (usually subclinical)
4. Chronic immune response causes liquefaction → air pockets for aerobic bacterial growth
5. Rupture of tubercles → **miliary tuberculosis** (dissemination via capillaries to all organs)
6. Healed lesions form **calcified Ghon complexes** (visible on chest radiographs)
7. Viable bacteria remain sequestered → **reactivation tuberculosis** in immunocompromised individuals (including HIV, malnutrition, immunosuppressants)

**Diagnosis**:
- Mantoux tuberculin skin test (TST): 0.1 mL PPD intradermal; read at 48–72 hours; ≥10 mm = positive. Limitations: BCG vaccination causes false-positives; immunosuppressed may have false-negatives.
- Chest radiographs: Confirm Ghon complex formation; apical infiltrates in reactivation TB
- Sputum AFB smear and culture (weeks for culture)
- QuantiFERON-TB Gold (IGRA): Blood test; not affected by BCG; preferred for BCG-vaccinated individuals

**Treatment**: Long-term multidrug therapy to prevent resistance.
- **RIPE** (standard 6-month regimen):
  - **R**ifampin (RIF): 6 months
  - **I**soniazid (INH): 6 months (+ pyridoxine B6)
  - **P**yrazinamide (PZA): First 2 months
  - **E**thambutol (EMB): First 2 months
- MDR-TB: Requires 18–24 months of second-line drugs
- XDR-TB: May require bedaquiline, linezolid; limited options

**Vaccination**: BCG (bacillus Calmette-Guérin from *M. bovis*) used globally; limited use in US (interferes with TST interpretation).

#### Pertussis (Whooping Cough)

**Pathogen**: *Bordetella pertussis* — gram-negative coccobacillus transmitted by respiratory droplets.

**Virulence Factors**:
- Filamentous hemagglutinin adhesin for epithelial attachment
- Pertussis toxin (PT): A-B exotoxin increasing cAMP, disrupting cellular signaling, enhancing histamine/serotonin inflammation
- Tracheal cytotoxin: Damages ciliated epithelial cells → mucus accumulation → secondary infections

**Clinical Stages**:
1. **Catarrhal** (week 1-2): Mild nasal congestion, runny nose, sneezing, low-grade fever. Most infectious.
2. **Paroxysmal** (weeks 2-6): "Uncontrollable coughing spasms lasting several minutes"; characteristic "whoop" from rapid inhalation at end of coughing fit; frequently induces vomiting.
3. **Convalescence**: Chronic cough lasting months ("100-day cough").

**Complications**: In infants, coughing causes rib fractures; can be fatal.

**Treatment**: Self-limiting; antibiotics (erythromycin, tetracycline, azithromycin) effective only in early catarrhal stage; reduce transmission if given later.

**Prevention**: DTaP (children), Tdap (adolescents/adults, including pregnant women 27-36 weeks gestation for passive protection of newborn).

#### Legionnaires Disease

**Pathogen**: *Legionella pneumophila* — aerobic gram-negative bacillus; infects free-living amoebae in moist environments (cooling towers, humidifiers, decorative fountains, hospital water systems).

**Pathophysiology**: Bacteria phagocytized by macrophages but "uses a secretion system to insert proteins in the endosomal membrane" preventing lysosomal fusion → continues proliferating within phagosome.

**Clinical**: Mild to severe pneumonia; fever, nausea, vomiting, neurological effects possible; ~10% mortality.

**Diagnosis**: Fastidious organism, difficult to culture. **Urinary antigen test** (>90% sensitivity/specificity for serotype 1) — rapid, diagnostic. Sputum isolation remains definitive. Not efficiently Gram-stained (Warthin-Starry silver-precipitate procedure used).

**Treatment**: Fluoroquinolones and macrolides effective. No vaccine available.

### 22.3 Viral Infections of the Respiratory Tract

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- "Identify the most common viruses that can cause infections of the upper and lower respiratory tract"
- "Compare the major characteristics of specific viral diseases of the respiratory tract"

#### Common Cold
- Over 200 viruses; primarily rhinoviruses, coronaviruses, and adenoviruses
- Rhinoviruses replicate optimally at 33–35°C (cooler nasal temperature vs. body 37°C)
- Self-limiting, typically resolving within 1–2 weeks
- No effective antivirals; antibacterial drugs contraindicated unless secondary bacterial infection confirmed

#### Influenza

**Causative Agent**: Orthomyxovirus with segmented RNA genome (7–8 segments).

**Key Proteins**: 
- **Hemagglutinin (H)**: Enables viral attachment to host cells with sialic acid receptors; enables entry
- **Neuraminidase (N)**: Enables viral release from host cells; prevents clumping

**Types**:
- **Influenza A**: Most virulent; has animal reservoirs; causes seasonal epidemics and pandemics
- **Influenza B**: Moderate severity; no significant animal reservoirs; occasionally epidemic
- **Influenza C**: Mildest symptoms; sporadic cases

**Antigenic Variation**:
- **Antigenic Drift**: Gradual mutations in H and N proteins from RNA polymerase errors → new strains annually → annual vaccine updates needed
- **Antigenic Shift**: Recombination producing novel H and N combinations when two different influenza viruses infect same host (e.g., pig) simultaneously → novel pandemic strains; no population immunity

**Historical Pandemics**:
- 1918–1919 Spanish flu (H1N1): 20–40 million deaths
- 1957–1958 Asian flu (H2N2): 1–2 million deaths
- 1968–1969 Hong Kong flu (H3N2): 1–3 million deaths
- 2009–2010 Swine flu (H1N1/09): 152,000–575,000 deaths

**Complications**: Pneumonia, Reye syndrome (children using aspirin), cytokine storm in severe cases, fatal outcomes.

**Treatment**: Neuraminidase inhibitors (oseltamivir/Tamiflu, zanamivir/Relenza) shorten disease course if taken within first 48 hours of symptom onset.

**Vaccination**: Annual vaccines; approximately 40–60% effectiveness varying by strain match.

#### Measles (Rubeola)
- Measles virus — single-stranded RNA; hemagglutinin spike proteins
- Extremely contagious; airborne
- Initial: High fever, conjunctivitis, sore throat; then characteristic red macular rash (face → trunk → extremities)
- **Koplik's spots**: White spots on inner cheek lining — pathognomonic (diagnostic) sign
- Measles virus suppresses immune system → predisposes to secondary infections
- Complications: Pneumonia, encephalitis, death; 10–15% fatality in severe strains
- 145,000+ deaths worldwide in 2013
- Prevention: MMR vaccine; <200 cases annually in US

#### Rubella (German Measles)
- Rubella virus; enveloped RNA
- Nearly 50% remain asymptomatic; facial rash for 2–3 days; lower fever than measles; no Koplik's spots
- **CRITICAL**: Congenital rubella syndrome — virus is teratogenic; exposure before 11 weeks gestation causes very high stillbirth/birth defect rate
- Prevention: MMR vaccine; essentially eliminated in US

#### Chickenpox and Shingles
**Causative Agent**: Varicella-zoster virus (VZV) — herpesvirus family

**Chickenpox**: Respiratory tract infection → viremia → fever/chills → pustular rash (face → trunk → extremities; majority on trunk). Infectious from ~2 days before rash until all lesions scabbed.

**Latency**: Virus moves along sensory nerves to dorsal spinal ganglia → remains dormant for decades.

**Shingles (Herpes Zoster)**: Reactivation from stress, aging, or immunosuppression → painful face/trunk lesions (dermatomal distribution) → lasts 2–6 weeks; may recur.

**Postherpetic Neuralgia**: Chronic pain from damaged nerves persisting after symptom resolution.

**Treatment**: Acyclovir (or valacyclovir, famciclovir) for shingles reduces severity and postherpetic neuralgia risk.

**Prevention**: 
- Chickenpox vaccine: 2 doses (12–15 months; 4–6 years)
- Shingrix (RZV): 2 doses; adults ≥50 years; ~90%+ effective against shingles

**Cold vs. Influenza Comparison**:
| Feature | Common Cold | Influenza |
|---------|------------|-----------|
| Fever | Low/absent | High (39°C+) |
| Aches | Mild | Severe |
| Fatigue | Slight | Severe |
| Nasal congestion | Common | Rare |
| Sneezing | Common | Rare |
| Onset | Gradual | Sudden |

## CHAPTER 23: Urogenital System Infections

### 23.2 Bacterial Infections of the Urinary System

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- "Identify the most common bacterial pathogens that can cause urinary tract infections"
- "Compare the major characteristics of specific bacterial diseases affecting the urinary tract"

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Cystitis**: Bladder inflammation.

**Dysuria**: "Urination accompanied by burning, discomfort, or pain."

**Pyuria**: Pus in the urine.

**Hematuria**: Blood in the urine.

**Pyelonephritis**: "An inflammation of the kidney."

**Glomerulonephritis**: Condition where glomeruli of nephrons are damaged from inflammation.

#### Epidemiology
More prevalent in females due to anatomical factors: "the urethra is short and located in close proximity to the anus, which can result in infections of the urinary tract by fecal bacteria." Also: shorter urethra, proximity to vaginal flora.

Elderly populations: Increased risk due to incomplete bladder emptying and weakened immunity. UTIs in elderly may present as acute confusion/delirium without classic urinary symptoms.

#### Common Causative Organisms
**Gram-negative** (most common):
- *E. coli*: Most common UTI pathogen overall (~80% of uncomplicated UTIs)
- *Proteus mirabilis*: Urease-producing → raises urine pH → struvite stones
- *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*: Hospital-associated; antibiotic-resistant
- *Klebsiella pneumoniae*

**Gram-positive**:
- *Staphylococcus saprophyticus*: Second most common in young sexually active women
- *Enterococcus faecalis*
- *Streptococcus agalactiae* (Group B Strep)

#### Diagnostic Methods

**Urine Dipstick Testing**: Test strips detect presence of:
- **Nitrites**: "The presence of nitrite may indicate the presence of *E. coli* or *K. pneumoniae*; these bacteria produce nitrate reductase, which converts nitrate to nitrite."
- **Leukocyte Esterase (LE)**: Detects neutrophils indicating active infection
- Protein or blood

**Clean Catch Technique**: Washing genital area, releasing initial urine, then collecting mid-stream specimen to minimize contamination.

**Urine Culture and Sensitivity**: Gold standard; counts colonies ≥100,000 CFU/mL = significant bacteriuria; identifies organism and antibiotic sensitivities.

#### Treatment
- Uncomplicated UTI: Fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin), nitrofurantoin, cephalosporins, TMP-SMX
- Note: increasing fluoroquinolone resistance; TMP-SMX resistance >20% in many communities → check local antibiogram
- Pain management: Phenazopyridine (urinary analgesic; turns urine orange — warn patients)

#### Pyelonephritis
Develops from bacteria spreading from lower urinary tract or via bloodstream. Key symptoms: flank/back pain, costovertebral angle (CVA) tenderness, fever, nausea/vomiting, chills.

"Gross hematuria (visible blood in the urine) occurs in 30–40% of females but is rare in males."

Complications: Bacteremia, kidney scarring causing dysfunction.

Treatment: Fluoroquinolones (oral, if outpatient); IV antibiotics (hospitalized/severe).

#### Glomerulonephritis
Most recognized mechanism: "Post-streptococcal sequelae associated with *Streptococcus pyogenes* throat and skin infections." *S. pyogenes* doesn't directly infect glomeruli; "immune complexes that form in blood between *S. pyogenes* antigens and antibodies lodge in the capillary endothelial cell junctions of the glomeruli and trigger a damaging inflammatory response."

### 23.3 Bacterial Infections of the Reproductive System (STIs)

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- "Identify the most common bacterial pathogens that can cause infections of the reproductive system"
- "Compare the major characteristics of specific bacterial diseases affecting the reproductive system"

#### Gonorrhea
**Pathogen**: *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* — gram-negative diplococcus

**Virulence Factors**: Fimbriae for epithelial attachment; lipooligosaccharide endotoxin.

**Male Symptoms**: Pain/burning during urination; yellow, green, or white penile discharge; testicular swelling; rectal discharge, soreness, bleeding, itching.

**Female Symptoms**: Pelvic pain, vaginal discharge, intermenstrual bleeding, dysuria, rectal symptoms. Often asymptomatic → undiagnosed.

**Serious Complications**:
- Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) → salpingitis → infertility through fallopian tube scarring
- Ectopic pregnancy risk
- Disseminated gonococcal infection (DGI): Bacteremia → endocarditis, septic arthritis, meningitis

**Diagnostic Tests**: Urine NAAT (nucleic acid amplification test) — most sensitive and specific; culture; ELISA.

**Treatment**: Ceftriaxone 500 mg IM (single dose) — PLUS azithromycin (if C. trachomatis not excluded). CDC-recommended due to frequent co-infection.

**Antibiotic Resistance**: Strains show resistance to fluoroquinolones, cefixime, azithromycin, tetracycline. CDC classifies as urgent threat.

#### Chlamydia
**Pathogen**: *Chlamydia trachomatis* — gram-negative obligate intracellular bacterium (cannot be cultured on standard media)

**Overview**: Major cause of nongonococcal urethritis (NGU); #1 most reported STI in the US. Often asymptomatic (70-80% of women).

**Male Manifestations**: Nongonococcal urethritis, epididymitis, orchitis.

**Female Manifestations**: Urethritis, salpingitis, pelvic inflammatory disease; associated with increased risk of cervical cancer.

**Lymphogranuloma Venereum (LGV)**: Certain serovars cause infection of lymphatic system in groin → "buboes (large lymph nodes)" that may burst. Common in tropical regions; co-occurs with HIV.

**Screening**: Routine screening recommended for all sexually active females under 25, high-risk individuals, or those beginning prenatal care.

**Diagnostic Tests**: NAAT — urine samples or vaginal/cervical swabs.

**Treatment**: Azithromycin 1g (single dose) or doxycycline 100mg BID × 7 days (CDC-recommended).

**Perinatal Complications**: *Chlamydia* and *Gonorrhea* exposure through birth canal → neonatal conjunctivitis; *Chlamydia* → neonatal pneumonia. Prevented by erythromycin eye ointment at birth.

#### Syphilis
**Pathogen**: *Treponema pallidum* — gram-negative spirochete; cannot be cultured in vitro.

**Disease Progression — Four Stages**:

**Primary Syphilis** (10–90 days post-transmission):
- Single lesion: **hard chancre** on cervix, penis, anus, or mouth
- "Initially hard and painless" → ulcerated sore
- Contains abundant *T. pallidum* (highly infectious)
- Possible localized lymph node swelling
- Heals spontaneously in 2–6 weeks; often unnoticed

**Secondary Syphilis** (weeks to months after primary):
- Rash affecting skin and mucous membranes
- Rash begins on **palms/soles** (distinctive!) → spreads to trunk/limbs
- Forms: macular, papular, mucus patches, or condylomata lata (white, wartlike lesions)
- Malaise, fever, lymph node swelling
- Highly contagious; lasts 2–6 weeks; recurrent in ~25% of cases

**Latent Phase**:
- No symptoms; high microbial levels persist
- Blood tests detect disease; can persist for years

**Tertiary Syphilis** (10–20 years post-infection):
- Granulomatous lesions called **gummas** in mucous membranes, bones, internal organs
- "Can be large and destructive, potentially causing massive tissue damage"
- **Cardiovascular syphilis**: Aortic aneurysm, coronary stenosis
- **Neurosyphilis**: Dementia, personality changes, seizures, paralysis, speech impairment, loss of vision/hearing, loss of bowel/bladder control

**Diagnostic Methods**:
- Darkfield/brightfield microscopy of lesion exudate (gold standard for primary)
- Serology (both types recommended for complete workup):
  - **Nontreponemal**: VDRL, RPR (rapid plasma reagin) — detect nonspecific antibodies; used for screening and monitoring treatment response
  - **Treponemal**: TP-PA, FTA-ABS — measure *T. pallidum* antibodies; confirmatory; remain positive for life

**Treatment**: Parenteral penicillin G benzathine (preferred). Alternatives for penicillin allergy: tetracycline, doxycycline (but NOT in pregnancy — desensitize and use penicillin).

**Congenital Syphilis**: Transmitted during pregnancy with untreated primary/secondary syphilis; causes miscarriage, stillbirth, or secondary syphilis symptoms in infants. TORCH prenatal screening.

#### Bacterial Vaginosis (BV)
**Primary Causative Agent**: *Gardnerella vaginalis*; also anaerobic species.

**Pathogenesis**: Decrease in *Lactobacillus* → rise in vaginal pH → *G. vaginalis* forms biofilm + vaginolysin lyses epithelial cells.

**Symptoms**: Thin white-to-yellow homogeneous discharge; burning; fishy odor; itching. Often asymptomatic.

**Diagnostic Criteria (Amsel's Criteria — 3 of 4)**:
1. White-yellow discharge
2. Fishy odor with KOH (positive "whiff test")
3. pH >4.5
4. Clue cells present (epithelial cells with stippled/granular appearance on Gram stain)

**Treatment**: Clindamycin, metronidazole, tinidazole.

**Pregnancy Risk**: BV increases risk of preterm birth, low birth weight, second-trimester miscarriage.

## CHAPTER 24: Digestive System Infections

### 24.3 Bacterial Infections of the Gastrointestinal Tract

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- Identify common GI tract bacteria
- Compare characteristics of specific bacterial diseases affecting the GI tract

#### Key Concepts

**Foodborne Disease Distinction**:
- **Infection**: Microbial agent colonizes gut, then produces toxins in host
- **Intoxication**: Bacteria produce toxins in food BEFORE ingestion; rapid onset without infection

#### Major Bacterial GI Pathogens

**Staphylococcal Food Poisoning**:
- *S. aureus* produces heat-stable enterotoxins in food
- Onset: 1–6 hours (rapid — intoxication, not infection)
- Symptoms: Nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, diarrhea
- Associated with undercooked meats, dairy, contaminated food handlers
- Self-limiting (24–48 hours); hydration; no antibiotics needed

**Salmonellosis** (*Salmonella enterica*):
- Penetrates epithelial cells via M cells → inflammatory response
- Fever, nausea, vomiting, cramps, diarrhea lasting days to weeks
- Associated with raw eggs/poultry, reptiles
- Estimated 1,000,000 cases annually in US; 380 deaths/year
- Usually self-limiting; antibiotics for severe/systemic cases or immunocompromised

**Typhoid Fever** (*Salmonella* Typhi):
- Severe systemic disease; "untreated mortality rate of 10%"
- "High fever, body aches, headache, nausea, lethargy, and possible rash" (rose spots on abdomen)
- Asymptomatic carriers shed bacteria via feces
- Complications: Intestinal perforation, pneumonia, jaundice
- Treatment: Fluoroquinolones or cephalosporins (check sensitivities — increasing resistance)
- Historical case: "Typhoid Mary" Mallon — asymptomatic carrier who transmitted 51 cases

**Shigellosis (Bacillary Dysentery)**:
- Gram-negative rods (*Shigella* species) invade intestinal epithelial cells
- "Abdominal cramps, fever, and watery diarrhea" sometimes bloody with mucus
- Very low infectious dose (<100 organisms)
- Complications: Hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS), reactive arthritis, irritable bowel syndrome
- Treatment: Azithromycin or fluoroquinolones (increasing resistance)
- Antibiotics shorten duration and reduce transmission

**Enterohemorrhagic *E. coli* (EHEC) — *E. coli* O157:H7**:
- Acquired Shiga toxin gene from *Shigella* via prophage (horizontal gene transfer)
- Produces Shiga-like toxin via prophage integration
- "Bloody diarrhea with severe cramping, but NO fever" — hallmark
- Very low infectious dose; associated with undercooked beef, leafy greens, unpasteurized milk
- Risk of **Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (HUS)**: Shiga toxin damages kidney endothelium → microangiopathic hemolytic anemia + thrombocytopenia + acute renal failure
- **ANTIBIOTICS CONTRAINDICATED** — increases Shiga toxin release → worsens HUS risk
- Treatment: Supportive care, hydration; monitor kidney function

**Enterotoxigenic *E. coli* (ETEC)** — "Traveler's Diarrhea":
- Heat-stable enterotoxin; self-limiting watery diarrhea and cramping
- Treatment: Hydration; azithromycin or rifaximin for severe cases

**Cholera** (*Vibrio cholerae*):
- Releases cholera toxin activating adenylate cyclase → increases cAMP → massive ion/water secretion
- "Rice water stool" — massive diarrhea (up to 1 liter/hour) → fatal dehydration
- Estimated 3–5 million cases and 100,000 deaths/year globally
- Treatment: **Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT)** — fluid and electrolyte replacement is life-saving; antibiotics (azithromycin or doxycycline) reduce duration

**Campylobacter jejuni**:
- Spiral gram-negative bacteria; most common bacterial cause of diarrhea in US
- Associated with undercooked poultry and unpasteurized milk
- Fever, diarrhea, cramps, sometimes dysentery
- Produces hemolysin and cytolethal distending toxin (DNase)
- Linked to Guillain-Barré syndrome (post-infectious peripheral neuropathy)
- Treatment: Usually self-limiting; azithromycin for severe cases

***Helicobacter pylori*** (Peptic Ulcers):
- Tolerates stomach acidity via urease (neutralizes acid) and motility
- Colonizes gastric epithelial cells; produces VacA toxin → vacuoles in epithelial cells → ulcers
- "Approximately two-thirds of the population is infected" but "less than 20% develop ulcers or stomach cancer"
- Found in ~80% stomach ulcers and >90% duodenal ulcers
- Risk factors for ulcers: NSAIDs (reduce mucosal protection), smoking, stress
- Increased risk of **gastric adenocarcinoma** and MALT lymphoma
- Diagnosis: Urea breath test (non-invasive), stool antigen test, endoscopic biopsy with CLO test
- Treatment: Triple therapy — **OAC regimen**: Omeprazole + Amoxicillin + Clarithromycin × 10–14 days; or quadruple therapy if clarithromycin resistance suspected

***Clostridioides difficile* (*C. diff*)**:
- Gram-positive rod; obligate anaerobe; endospore-forming
- Opportunistic pathogen when normal microbiota disrupted by antibiotics (especially clindamycin, cephalosporins, fluoroquinolones)
- Produces toxins **TcdA** (enterotoxin) and **TcdB** (cytotoxin): Inactivate GTP-binding proteins → actin condensation → cell death
- **Pseudomembranous colitis**: "Pseudomembrane of fibrin containing dead epithelial cells and leukocytes" covers colon
- Symptoms: Profuse watery diarrhea (≥3 stools/day), abdominal cramping, fever
- Complications: Toxic megacolon, colon perforation, septicemia, shock, death
- Risk factors: Immunocompromised status, extended healthcare stays, advanced age, recent antibiotics, proton pump inhibitors
- Diagnosis: Stool NAAT (PCR) for TcdB gene; enzyme immunoassay for toxins
- Treatment: Discontinue precipitating antibiotics; **vancomycin** (oral) or **fidaxomicin** preferred (metronidazole now second-line for mild disease per 2021 IDSA guidelines)
- Fecal microbiota transplant (FMT): ">90% success" in recurrent *C. diff* — restores gut microbiota
- Infection control: Spores resistant to alcohol-based hand sanitizers — must use **soap and water handwashing**; contact precautions; dedicated equipment

### 24.4 Viral Infections of the Gastrointestinal Tract

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- "Identify the most common viruses that can cause infections of the GI tract"
- "Compare the major characteristics of specific viral diseases affecting the GI tract and liver"

#### Rotavirus
- Double-stranded RNA virus
- "95% of children in the United States have had at least one rotavirus infection by the time they reach age five"
- Incubation ~2 days; fever, vomiting, diarrhea; lasts 3–8 days
- Risk of severe dehydration and malnutrition
- Diagnosis: Enzyme immunoassay of fecal samples
- Treatment: Supportive oral rehydration therapy
- Prevention: WHO-recommended vaccine for infants (two doses)

#### Norovirus (Norwalk Virus)
- Multiple strains; "known for causing widespread infections in groups of people in confined spaces, such as on cruise ships" and healthcare facilities
- Very low infectious dose; resistant to alcohol hand sanitizers
- Symptoms: Watery diarrhea, cramps, fever, projectile vomiting
- Incubation: 12–48 hours; duration: 1–2 days typically
- No specific medications; self-limiting; rehydration therapy recommended
- Infection control: Soap and water handwashing; bleach cleaning of surfaces

#### Viral Hepatitis — Summary Table
| Type | Genome | Transmission | Chronicity | Vaccine |
|------|--------|--------------|----------|---------|
| **HAV** | ssRNA (picornavirus) | Fecal-oral; contaminated food/water | No (usually) | Yes |
| **HBV** | dsDNA (hepadnavirus) | Blood, sexual, perinatal | Yes (90% of infected infants; 6–10% adults) | Yes |
| **HCV** | ssRNA (flavivirus) | Blood (IV drug use, transfusions) | Yes (75–85%) | No |
| **HDV** | ssRNA | Blood; requires HBV coinfection | Yes | HBV vaccine prevents |
| **HEV** | ssRNA | Fecal-oral (genotypes 1-2); zoonotic (3-4) | Rarely | China only |

**Hepatitis A (HAV)**:
- Non-enveloped ssRNA virus; fecal-oral route
- Incubation: 15–50 days (mean 30)
- Usually mild/asymptomatic; self-limiting within weeks
- Rarely causes fulminant hepatitis (70–80% fatality rate)
- Vaccine: Recommended ages 1–2 and high-risk adults; 2 doses

**Hepatitis B (HBV)**:
- Double-stranded DNA enveloped virus; uses reverse transcriptase
- Transmitted via infectious blood/body fluids (NOT casual contact, NOT breastfeeding)
- "Approximately 90% of infected infants become chronic carriers, compared with only 6–10% of infected adults"
- Chronic HBV → cirrhosis, liver failure, **hepatocellular carcinoma** (liver cancer)
- Vaccine: 3-dose series; recommended for all infants (dose at birth), children, and healthcare workers
- Post-exposure: Hepatitis B immunoglobulin (HBIG) + vaccine within 24 hours if unvaccinated

**Hepatitis C (HCV)**:
- "75%–85% of infected individuals become chronic carriers"
- Transmitted via blood (IV drug use, transfusions pre-1992, sharing personal items)
- Incubation: Mean 45 days
- Often asymptomatic initially; may resolve spontaneously
- Chronic HCV → cirrhosis, liver failure, hepatocellular carcinoma
- Treatment: Direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) — sofosbuvir-based regimens; >95% cure rate; genotype-specific regimens
- No vaccine available
- Universal screening: All adults 18–79 years (USPSTF recommendation)

**Common Hepatitis Symptoms**:
"Malaise, anorexia, loss of appetite, dark urine, pain in the upper right quadrant of the abdomen, vomiting, nausea, diarrhea, joint pain, and gray/clay-colored stool" + jaundice (yellowing of skin, sclera, mucous membranes from bilirubin buildup)

**Laboratory Testing for Hepatitis**:
- Liver function tests: ALT, AST, alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin, serum albumin
- Hepatitis virus serological test panel detects specific antibodies/antigens
- HBsAg (hepatitis B surface antigen) = current HBV infection
- Anti-HBs (antibody to HBsAg) = past infection or vaccination
- HBeAg = high viral replication/infectivity in HBV
- Anti-HCV = HCV exposure; confirm with HCV RNA PCR

**Healthcare Worker HBV Prevention**:
"Hepatitis B was once a leading on-the-job hazard for health-care workers." Prevention:
- Vaccination (recommended since 1982) — mandatory for healthcare workers
- Avoid body fluid contact via gloves and face protection
- Use disposable syringes/needles; never recap
- Wash exposed skin with soap and water immediately
- 10% bleach solution for surface disinfection
- Post-exposure: HBIG + vaccination within 24 hours
