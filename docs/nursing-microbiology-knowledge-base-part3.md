### 17.1 Physical Defenses

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- "Describe the various physical barriers and mechanical defenses that protect the human body against infection and disease"
- "Describe the role of microbiota as a first-line defense against infection and disease"

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Physical Barriers**: Structures preventing microbes from reaching susceptible tissues, including the skin, mucous membranes, and endothelial cells connected by tight junctions.

**Epidermis**: The thin upper layer of skin composed of keratin-packed dead cells that shed continuously.

**Dermis**: The thick middle skin layer containing hair follicles, sweat glands, nerves, and blood vessels.

**Hypodermis**: A fatty tissue layer beneath the dermis housing blood and lymph vessels.

**Mucous Membranes**: Epithelial cell linings secreting mucus to trap debris and microbes in respiratory, digestive, urinary, and reproductive tracts.

**Mucociliary Escalator**: A system where ciliated cells propel mucus-trapped debris outward from the lungs. Cigarette smoke destroys cilia, impairing this defense.

**Cilia**: "Hair-like appendages" on epithelial cells that propel debris-laden mucus.

**Goblet Cells**: Mucus-secreting intestinal cells interspersed among epithelial cells.

**Peristalsis**: Muscular contractions moving digestive tract contents toward excretion — mechanical removal of microbes.

**Endothelia**: Tightly packed epithelial cells lining blood vessels, lymphatic vessels, and urogenital tracts.

**Blood-Brain Barrier**: Tightly connected endothelial cells protecting the central nervous system from transient bloodstream microbes.

**Microbiome**: Resident microorganisms occupying binding sites and competing with pathogens for nutrients — competitive exclusion.

#### Core Content

**Three Strategies of Physical Defense**:

1. **Cellular Barriers** (skin, mucous membranes, endothelia): Deny pathogen entry.
   - Skin creates unfavorable conditions through fatty acids, producing a dry, salty, acidic environment.
   - Continuously sheds dead cells carrying adherent microbes.

2. **Mechanical Defenses** (shedding, mucociliary sweeping, peristalsis, urine/tear flow): Remove pathogens.
   - Mucociliary escalator expels respiratory debris upward for swallowing or expulsion.
   - Urine flow prevents urinary tract colonization.
   - Tear production and blinking remove ocular debris.

3. **Microbiome** (resident bacteria across body sites): Competitively excludes pathogens.
   - Vaginal bacteria (Lactobacillus) suppress *Candida* growth.
   - Antibiotic-induced microbiota depletion increases pathogenic colonization risk (*C. difficile* infections).
   - Fecal transplantation restores protective microbial communities.

#### Clinical Application
Nursing significance: Wound care, urinary catheters, and intubation all disrupt physical barriers. Patients on antibiotics are at risk for *C. difficile* colitis as normal gut flora are disrupted. Smoking damages the mucociliary escalator, predisposing to respiratory infections like pneumonia.

### 17.2 Chemical Defenses

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
1. "Describe how enzymes in body fluids provide protection against infection or disease"
2. "List and describe the function of antimicrobial peptides, complement components, cytokines, and acute-phase proteins"
3. "Describe similarities and differences among classic, alternate, and lectin complement pathways"

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Antimicrobial Peptides (AMPs)**: "Nonspecific cell-derived mediators with broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties" that damage membranes, destroy nucleic acids, or interfere with cell-wall synthesis.

**Defensins**: A family of AMPs produced by epithelial cells, macrophages, and neutrophils that combat microorganisms by damaging plasma membranes.

**Bacteriocins**: AMPs produced by gastrointestinal tract microbiota; often carried on plasmids.

**Acute-Phase Proteins**: "Primarily produced in the liver and secreted into the blood in response to inflammatory molecules." Examples: C-reactive protein (CRP), serum amyloid A, ferritin, fibrinogen, mannose-binding lectin. CRP is used clinically as a marker of inflammation.

**Complement System**: "Group of plasma protein mediators that can act as an innate nonspecific defense." More than 30 proteins (C1-C9) normally circulate as precursors.

**Opsonization**: "Coating of a pathogen by a chemical substance (called an opsonin) that allows phagocytic cells to recognize, engulf, and destroy it." Key opsonins: antibodies, complement C3b, C4b.

**Anaphylatoxins**: Complement fragments C3a and C5a with potent proinflammatory functions; cause vasodilation, recruit immune cells.

**Membrane Attack Complex (MAC)**: Structure formed by complement proteins C6-C9 that "punches through the cell membrane" → lysis of gram-negative bacteria.

**Cytokines**: "Soluble proteins that act as communication signals between cells."

**Interleukins**: Cytokines that modulate immune system functions; important in fever generation and leukocyte recruitment.

**Chemokines**: "Chemotactic factors that recruit leukocytes to sites of infection, tissue damage, and inflammation."

**Interferons**: Immune signaling molecules important for viral defense.
- **Type I** (interferon-α and interferon-β): Produced by virus-infected cells; inhibit mRNA production, destroy existing RNA, reduce protein synthesis; stimulate immune cells against infected cells.
- **Type II** (interferon-γ): Important immune cell activator; activates macrophages.

#### Chemical Defenses by Body Location
- **Skin**: Sebum (fatty acids); sweat (salt, lysozyme); resident microbiota produce oleic acid (acidic, hostile to pathogens)
- **Eyes**: Tears contain lysozyme (cleaves peptidoglycan bonds) and lactoferrin (sequesters iron, starving iron-dependent microbes)
- **Ears**: Cerumen (earwax) — antimicrobial through fatty acids; pH between 3 and 5
- **Respiratory Tract**: Lysozyme, lactoferrin, lactoperoxidase, surfactant (lipoprotein complex with antibacterial properties)
- **Digestive Tract**: Stomach acidity kills most microbes; bile; pancreatic enzymes; Paneth cells producing lysozyme
- **Urinary System**: Urine — slightly acidic pH (~6) inhibiting growth; physical flushing
- **Female Reproductive System**: Lactobacilli ferment glycogen to lactate → lower vaginal pH → inhibits pathogens including *Candida*

#### Three Complement Activation Pathways
All three converge at C3 cleavage to produce C3a and C3b.

**1. Alternative Pathway** (innate; no antibody needed):
- Initiated by spontaneous C3 hydrolysis
- C3b attaches to microbial surfaces, recruiting additional complement proteins

**2. Classical Pathway** (requires antibody):
- Requires antibody-antigen complex formation
- Activates C1 complex and cascading protein recruitment

**3. Lectin Pathway** (innate; no antibody needed):
- Triggered by mannose-binding lectin (acute-phase protein) binding microbial surface carbohydrates

**Common Protective Outcomes from All Three Pathways**:
- **Opsonization**: C1q, C3b, C4b coat pathogens → enhanced phagocytosis
- **Inflammation**: C3a and C5a anaphylatoxins → vasodilation, permeability increase
- **Chemotaxis**: C5a recruits neutrophils and white blood cells
- **Cytolysis**: MAC formation in gram-negative bacteria

Important limitation: **MAC is ineffective against gram-positive bacteria** (too thick cell wall). Complement-mediated opsonization remains crucial for their clearance.

#### Inflammation-Eliciting Mediators
- **Histamine**: Released by mast cells and basophils; promotes vasodilation, bronchoconstriction
- **Leukotrienes**: Lipid-based mediators; more potent and longer-lasting than histamine
- **Prostaglandins**: Promote inflammatory effects and fever; enhance white blood cell activity
- **Bradykinin**: Contributes to edema by increasing capillary dilation and permeability

### 17.3 Cellular Defenses

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- Identify and describe blood components
- Explain hematopoiesis (formed element formation)
- Describe characteristics of formed elements and their innate immune functions

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Hematopoiesis**: Process by which multipotent hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) in bone marrow differentiate into various blood cell types based on chemical signals.

**Formed Elements**: Blood cells derived from bone marrow stem cells: red blood cells (erythrocytes), white blood cells (leukocytes), platelets (thrombocytes).

**Granulocytes**: WBCs containing visible cytoplasmic granules: neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils.

**Agranulocytes**: WBCs lacking abundant granules: lymphocytes and monocytes.

#### Granulocytes (Detail)

**Neutrophils (PMNs — Polymorphonuclear Neutrophils)**:
- Most abundant leukocytes (50–70% of WBCs)
- Feature multilobed nuclei and lilac granules containing defensins and hydrolytic enzymes
- First responders to bacterial infection (arrive within hours)
- Functions: bacterial elimination via phagocytosis; "degranulation" (releasing toxic molecules); form **Neutrophil Extracellular Traps (NETs)** — chromatin meshes coupled with antimicrobial proteins for concentrated pathogen attacks
- Predominate in acute bacterial infections; elevated WBC count (leukocytosis) with "shift to left" (more band forms)

**Eosinophils**:
- Comprise 1–3% of leukocytes
- Two-lobed nuclei; bright red-orange granules containing histamine, degradative enzymes, and major basic protein (MBP)
- Combat protozoa and helminths; participate in allergic reactions
- Elevated in parasitic infections and allergic conditions

**Basophils**:
- Less than 1% of leukocytes
- Two-lobed nuclei; dark purple granules containing histamine
- Activated by complement fragments C3a and C5a
- Important in allergic and inflammatory responses

**Mast Cells**: Derived from myeloid progenitors; functionally similar to basophils but reside in tissues near blood vessels and mucous membranes rather than circulating. Key cells in allergic reactions and inflammation.

#### Agranulocytes (Detail)

**Natural Killer (NK) Cells)**:
- Nonspecific lymphocytes recognizing abnormal cells (cancer cells, virus-infected cells)
- Recognize decreased major histocompatibility complex (MHC) markers on target cells as "distress signals" ("missing self" mechanism)
- Kill targets via cytotoxic membrane proteins, cytokines, or perforin-mediated cytotoxicity (creating pores allowing granzyme entry triggering apoptosis)

**Monocytes**:
- Largest WBCs; lack lobes and granules
- Effective phagocytes
- Differentiate into tissue-specific **macrophages** and **dendritic cells** upon leaving circulation
- Macrophages: reside in lymphoid and nonlymphoid tissues; long-lived phagocytes; produce cytokines supporting immune responses; process and present antigens

### 17.4 Pathogen Recognition and Phagocytosis

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- "Explain how leukocytes migrate from peripheral blood into infected tissues"
- "Explain the mechanisms by which leukocytes recognize pathogens"
- "Explain the process of phagocytosis and the mechanisms by which phagocytes destroy pathogens"

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Phagocytosis**: The process where specialized cells seek, ingest, and eliminate pathogens. First observed in starfish during the 1880s by Nobel Prize-winning zoologist Ilya Metchnikoff.

**Extravasation (Diapedesis)**: "Movement of leukocytes from the bloodstream through capillary vessel walls into infected tissue." Process involves "rolling adhesion" along vessel walls before squeezing through cellular junctions via transendothelial migration. Does NOT occur in arteries or veins (turbulent flow, thick walls).

**Pathogen-Associated Molecular Patterns (PAMPs)**: Molecular structures common to many pathogenic microbes that phagocytes can recognize. Examples: peptidoglycan, flagellin, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), lipopeptides, viral nucleic acids.

**Pattern Recognition Receptors (PRRs)**: Cellular structures allowing phagocytes to detect PAMPs. **Toll-like receptors (TLRs)** are one important group.

**Phagosome**: Membrane vesicle formed when a phagocyte engulfs a pathogen using pseudopods.

**Phagolysosome**: Structure formed when a phagosome fuses with one or more lysosomes, creating an acidic environment (pH 4–5) containing digestive enzymes.

**Respiratory Burst**: Antimicrobial mechanism where phagocytes increase oxygen consumption to produce reactive oxygen species (superoxide anion, hydrogen peroxide, hydroxyl radicals). Creates a "killing zone" inside the phagolysosome.

**Opsonization**: Coating of pathogens with antibodies, complement factors (C1q, C3b, C4b), or lectins to enhance recognition and attachment by phagocytes.

#### Extravasation Process
1. Infection triggers resident macrophages/tissue cells to release C5a and proinflammatory cytokines
2. Signals trigger upregulation of cellular adhesion molecules on endothelial cells lining capillaries
3. Leukocytes slow, roll along vessel walls, bind to additional adhesion molecules
4. Leukocytes "squeeze through the cellular junction in a process known as transendothelial migration"
5. Neutrophils typically arrive within hours; monocytes may require several days to differentiate into macrophages

#### Phagocytosis Steps
1. **Engulfment**: Pseudopods wrap around pathogen, forming phagosome
2. **Acidification**: pH decreases to 4–5, providing early antibacterial activity
3. **Fusion**: Phagosome merges with lysosomes → phagolysosome
4. **Enzymatic Degradation**: Lysozyme, phospholipase, and proteases digest pathogen
5. **Respiratory Burst**: Production of reactive oxygen species (superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, hydroxyl radicals)
6. **Additional Mechanisms**: Peroxynitrite formation; release of microbicidal defensins from granules
7. **Antigen Processing**: Macrophages and dendritic cells process degraded pathogen remains and present antigens for adaptive immune responses
8. **Exocytosis**: Remaining waste products excreted

#### Clinical Connection: Leishmaniasis (When Phagocytosis Fails)
*Leishmania* protozoan transmitted by sand fly bites. After phagocytosis, *Leishmania* amastigotes survive through surface lipophosphoglycan and acid phosphatase that "inhibit the macrophage respiratory burst and lysosomal enzymes." Parasites multiply within macrophages, then lyse them and infect additional cells. ~1.3 million new infections annually worldwide.

### 17.5 Inflammation and Fever

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- Recognize inflammation and fever signs and explain their occurrence
- Discuss benefits and risks of inflammatory responses

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Inflammation (Inflammatory Response)**: "Cascade of chemical mediators and cellular responses" triggered by cell damage or pathogen breach.

**Five Observable Signs of Inflammation**:
1. **Erythema** (redness)
2. **Edema** (swelling)
3. **Heat**
4. **Pain**
5. **Altered function**

**Histamine**: Released by mast cells; causes vasodilation and increased vascular permeability.

**Bradykinin**: Maintains capillary dilation, leading to edema.

**Pus**: Accumulation of neutrophils, dead cells, tissue fluids, and lymph.

**Chronic Inflammation**: Ongoing lower-level battle when acute inflammation cannot clear pathogens.

**Granulomas**: "Pockets of infected tissue walled off and surrounded by WBCs." Example: tubercles in TB.

**Fever**: "Inflammatory response that extends beyond the site of infection" causing overall body temperature increase.

**Pyrogens**: Chemicals altering hypothalamus "thermostat setting" to elevate body temperature.
- **Exogenous pyrogens**: External sources (e.g., lipopolysaccharide from gram-negative bacteria)
- **Endogenous pyrogens**: Internal sources (interleukin-1, IL-6, interferon-γ, tumor necrosis factor α)

**Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2)**: Molecule released from cells, resetting hypothalamus to initiate fever. NSAIDs (aspirin, ibuprofen) work by blocking PGE2 synthesis.

**Superantigens**: "Bacterial or viral proteins" causing excessive T cell activation and cytokine release → dangerous fevers.

#### Acute Inflammation Process
1. Brief vasoconstriction minimizing blood loss
2. Vasodilation and permeability increase → fluids flood tissues → erythema, edema, heat
3. Histamine release from resident mast cells → recruits phagocytes and immune cells
4. Complement system activation produces C5a → further enhances response
5. Bradykinin maintains capillary dilation
6. Neutrophil recruitment increases → pus formation
7. Macrophages eventually clear pus after several days → tissue repair

#### Chronic Inflammation
When acute inflammation fails eliminating pathogens, chronic inflammation results. Associated with:
- Degenerative neurological diseases (Alzheimer's, Parkinson's)
- Heart disease
- Metastatic cancer
- Tuberculosis (tubercle/granuloma formation in lung tissue)
- Hepatitis C (liver scarring, cirrhosis)

#### Fever Mechanism
1. Exogenous pyrogens (e.g., LPS from gram-negative bacteria) activate leukocytes
2. Leukocytes release endogenous pyrogens (IL-1, IL-6, IFN-γ, TNF)
3. These trigger circumventricular organs producing PGE2
4. Hypothalamus resets to higher temperature set point
5. Shivering and vasoconstriction raise body temperature to new set point
6. Negative feedback: temperature-dependent inhibition decreases cytokine production

**Fever Advantages**:
- Stimulates leukocyte pathogen-killing activity
- Inhibits pathogen growth (human pathogens optimized for ~35°C)
- May stimulate liver iron-sequestering compounds, starving iron-dependent microbes

**Fever Risks**:
- Excessive fever from superantigen-induced responses causes life-threatening tissue and organ damage.
- *S. aureus* and *S. pyogenes* produce superantigens causing toxic shock syndrome (fevers exceeding 42°C/108°F).

#### Physiological Changes During Fever
- **Rising phase**: Skin appears pale from vasoconstriction; shivering generates heat
- **Crisis phase**: Hypothalamus stimulates vasodilation and sweating, releasing body heat ("breaking the fever")

### 18.1 Overview of Specific Adaptive Immunity

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- Define memory, primary response, secondary response, and specificity
- Distinguish between humoral and cellular immunity
- Differentiate between antigens, epitopes, and haptens
- Describe antibody structure and function; distinguish antibody classes

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Adaptive Immunity**: System characterized by specificity (targeting specific pathogens) and memory (rapid response to previously encountered pathogens). Takes days to weeks to mount initially; much faster on re-exposure.

**Primary Response**: Initial immune reaction triggered by first pathogen exposure or vaccination.

**Secondary Response**: Faster, stronger immune reaction upon re-exposure to a pathogen, resulting from immunological memory. Forms the basis of vaccination.

**Humoral Immunity**: "Mechanisms of adaptive specific immunity that involve B cells and antibody production" — targets extracellular pathogens and toxins.

**Cellular Immunity**: Targeting and destruction of intracellular pathogen-infected cells by T cells.

**Antigens**: Pathogen-specific molecular structures triggering adaptive immunity; unique to specific pathogens.

**Epitopes**: "Smaller exposed regions on the surface of antigens" recognized by antibodies and T cells.

**Haptens**: Small molecules that are "essentially free epitopes" — require attachment to carrier proteins to become immunogenic. Example: penicillin can act as a hapten, triggering allergic reactions.

**Antibodies / Immunoglobulins**: "Glycoproteins that are present in both the blood and tissue fluids." Y-shaped structure with antigen-binding sites at arms' tips.

**Fab Region**: "Fragment of antigen binding" containing variable regions for antigen binding.

**Fc Region**: "Fragment of crystallization" — site for complement and phagocyte binding.

#### Antibody Classes

| Class | Structure | Location | Key Functions |
|-------|-----------|----------|--------------|
| **IgG** | Monomer | Blood/tissues | 80% serum antibodies; crosses placenta (passive immunity to fetus); most abundant; long-lived |
| **IgM** | Pentamer | Blood | 6% serum; FIRST produced in immune response; excellent agglutinator; activates complement |
| **IgA** | Dimer | Mucous secretions (saliva, tears, breast milk, GI) | 13% serum; traps pathogens in mucus; most prevalent in secretions |
| **IgD** | Monomer | B cell surfaces | <1% serum; B cell receptor; role in B cell activation |
| **IgE** | Monomer | Blood | <1% serum; anti-parasitic defense; mediates allergic reactions (binds to mast cells) |

#### Antigen-Antibody Functions
1. **Neutralization**: "Binding of certain antibodies to epitopes on the surface of pathogens or toxins, preventing their attachment to cells."
2. **Opsonization**: Coating pathogens to enhance phagocyte recognition and engulfment.
3. **Agglutination**: Cross-linking pathogens into large aggregates for easier removal.
4. **Complement Activation**: Classical pathway requiring IgG or IgM binding to pathogen surfaces → complement cascade → opsonization, inflammation, lysis.
5. **Antibody-Dependent Cell-Mediated Cytotoxicity (ADCC)**: NK cells and other effector cells kill large pathogens (helminths) coated with antibodies.

### 18.3 T Lymphocytes and Cellular Immunity

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- Describe T-cell maturation and thymic selection processes
- Describe characteristics and functions of different T-cell classes
- Explain how superantigens trigger uncontrolled T-cell activation

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Cellular Immunity**: Immune response targeting intracellular pathogens through T lymphocyte actions.

**T Lymphocytes (T cells)**: White blood cells responsible for cell-mediated immunity; mature in the thymus.

**Thymic Selection**: Process of positive and negative selection eliminating defective thymocytes in the thymus. Approximately "98% of thymocytes" are eliminated; remaining 2% become mature naïve T cells.

**Central Tolerance**: "Prevents self-reacting T cells from reaching the bloodstream and potentially causing autoimmune disease."

**Anergy**: "A state of nonresponsiveness to antigen stimulation" — mechanism of peripheral tolerance.

**CD Molecules**: "Cell surface glycoproteins that can be used to identify and distinguish between the various types of white blood cells."

**T-Cell Receptor (TCR)**: Epitope-specific receptor with variable and constant regions enabling antigen recognition. Diversity generated by combining V, D, and J gene segments → ~25 million unique TCRs.

**MHC I (Major Histocompatibility Complex Class I)**: Present on ALL nucleated cells; presents INTRACELLULAR antigens (viruses, intracellular bacteria) to CD8+ cytotoxic T cells.

**MHC II**: Present on professional antigen-presenting cells (macrophages, dendritic cells, B cells); presents EXTRACELLULAR antigens to CD4+ helper T cells.

#### Classes of T Cells

**Helper T Cells (CD4+)**:
- Activated by APCs presenting antigens with MHC II
- "Orchestrate humoral and cellular immunity"
- Enhance macrophage and NK cell functions
- Secrete cytokines that activate B cells and cytotoxic T cells
- HIV infects and destroys CD4+ T cells → AIDS

**Subtypes of Helper T Cells**:
- **TH1 Cells**: Stimulate cytotoxic T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and NK cells for enhanced intracellular pathogen killing
- **TH2 Cells**: "Orchestrate antibody class switching, which allows B cells to switch between the production of IgM, IgG, IgA, and IgE"; fight parasites
- **TH17 Cells**: Target chronic mucocutaneous infections; patients lacking TH17 show increased susceptibility to bacteremia and GI infections
- **Memory Helper T Cells**: "Remember" specific pathogens, mounting rapid secondary responses

**Regulatory T Cells (CD4+)**:
- Prevent undesirable immune responses
- Enable peripheral tolerance
- Inhibit self-reactive T cell activation → prevent autoimmunity

**Cytotoxic T Cells (CD8+)**:
- Recognize antigens via MHC I presentation
- "Primary effector cells for cellular immunity"
- Destroy infected cells using **perforin** (creates target cell pores) and **granzymes** (proteases inducing apoptosis)
- Critical for eliminating virus-infected cells and cancer cells

#### Three-Step T Cell Activation Process
1. TCR recognition of foreign epitope within MHC cleft
2. CD4 (helper) or CD8 (cytotoxic) interaction anchoring complex
3. Cytokine secretion activating proliferation → clonal expansion

#### Superantigens and Unregulated T Cell Activation
**Mechanism**: "Simultaneous binding to MHC II molecules of APCs and the variable region of the TCR β chain" occurring OUTSIDE antigen-binding clefts, bypassing normal recognition.

**Result**: "Excessive, uncontrolled release of cytokines, often called a cytokine storm, which stimulates an excessive inflammatory response" → dangerous blood pressure drops, shock, multi-organ failure, potential death.

Examples:
- *S. aureus* Toxic Shock Syndrome Toxin (TSST-1), staphylococcal enterotoxins
- *S. pyogenes* streptococcal pyrogenic toxins

**Clinical Case: Toxic Shock Syndrome**: 22-year-old woman with sudden fever, vomiting, diarrhea, muscle aches progressing to hypotension and confusion. Diagnosis: TSS from TSST-1 superantigen associated with improper tampon use. Treatment: rehydration, antibiotics, supportive therapy, blood pressure management.

### 18.4 B Lymphocytes and Humoral Immunity

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
1. "Describe the production and maturation of B cells"
2. "Compare the structure of B-cell receptors and T-cell receptors"
3. "Compare T-dependent and T-independent activation of B cells"
4. "Compare the primary and secondary antibody responses"

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Humoral Immunity**: Adaptive immune defenses mediated by antibodies secreted by B lymphocytes.

**B Cells (B Lymphocytes)**: Lymphocytes originating from hematopoietic stem cells in bone marrow; mature in the bone marrow (unlike T cells, which mature in the thymus).

**B-Cell Receptors (BCRs)**: Membrane-bound monomeric forms of IgD and IgM; approximately 100,000 BCRs exist per naïve mature B cell. Unlike TCRs, BCRs can bind free antigens without MHC presentation.

**Plasma Cells**: Differentiated B cells that function as "antibody factories" secreting large quantities of antibodies after activation.

**Memory B Cells**: B cell clones programmed to respond quickly to subsequent exposures to the same antigen.

**T-Dependent Antigens**: Protein antigens requiring helper T cell cooperation for B cell activation → produce memory B cells and class switching.

**T-Independent Antigens**: Non-protein antigens (polysaccharides, lipopolysaccharides) that activate B cells without T cell involvement → produce IgM only, NO memory B cells.

**Class Switching (Isotype Switching)**: Process where cytokines from TH2 cells stimulate plasma cells to switch from IgM production to IgG, IgA, or IgE production while retaining original antigen specificity.

#### BCR vs. TCR Key Differences
1. BCRs interact with free antigens or pathogen-surface epitopes WITHOUT MHC presentation; TCRs REQUIRE MHC I or MHC II presentation
2. BCRs recognize diverse molecular classes (proteins, polysaccharides, lipopolysaccharides); TCRs only recognize protein epitopes

#### Primary and Secondary Antibody Responses

**Primary Response Timeline**:
- "Lag period" (latent period): ~10 days with no detectable serum antibody
- Peak IgM: ~14 days post-exposure
- IgM decline coincides with class switching initiation
- IgG peak: ~3 weeks post-exposure

**Secondary Response Characteristics**:
- Shortened lag period (few days only)
- Significantly elevated IgG production
- Higher-affinity antibodies binding targeted epitopes
- Longer-lived plasma cells maintaining elevated antibody levels

This is WHY booster vaccines work and why re-infection causes milder disease.

#### Clinical Significance: Maternal Antibody Transfer
- **IgG** crosses the placenta → provides passive immunity to newborns
- **IgA** (secretory) in breast milk → protects infant GI tract
- Maternal IgG wanes by ~6 months of age → why infant vaccinations are timed to this window

### 18.5 Vaccines

#### Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, students will be able to:
- "Compare the various kinds of artificial immunity"
- "Differentiate between variolation and vaccination"
- "Describe different types of vaccines and explain their respective advantages and disadvantages"

#### Key Terms and Definitions

**Vaccination**: A form of artificial immunity that stimulates adaptive immune defenses through deliberate exposure to weakened/inactivated pathogens or their antigens, triggering memory cell production without initial infection.

**Natural Active Immunity**: Develops following natural pathogen exposure; can provide lifelong protection.

**Natural Passive Immunity**: Maternal antibody transfer via placenta (IgG) or breast milk (secretory IgA); lasts ~6 months postnatally.

**Artificial Passive Immunity**: Transfer of donor-produced antibodies for post-exposure prophylaxis or active infection treatment. Examples: rabies immunoglobulin, hepatitis B immunoglobulin (HBIG). Provides immediate but short-lived protection (no memory cells generated).

**Herd Immunity**: Population protection occurring when sufficient immunity percentage reduces disease transmission, even among unvaccinated individuals. Varies by disease: measles requires ~95% immunity; polio requires ~80%.

**Attenuated**: Weakened pathogen virulence through genetic manipulation or culturing in unnatural environments.

#### Vaccine Classes

| Type | Mechanism | Examples | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|------|-----------|---------|-----------|--------------|
| **Live Attenuated** | Weakened pathogen; establishes subclinical infection | MMR, chickenpox, BCG, oral polio | Strong, long-lasting immunity; activates both humoral and cellular | Storage challenges; contraindicated in immunocompromised; rare reversion to virulence |
| **Inactivated** | Killed pathogen | Influenza (injected), HAV, rabies, IPV | Stable; safe for immunocompromised | Weaker humoral-only response; multiple boosters needed; higher doses |
| **Subunit** | Isolated pathogenic antigens | HBV, HPV, pertussis component | Minimal side effects; stable | Weaker immune response; adjuvants often needed |
| **Toxoid** | Inactivated bacterial toxins | Diphtheria, tetanus (in DTaP) | Activates humoral immunity for toxin neutralization | Boosters required (every 10 years for Td) |
| **Conjugate** | Protein-conjugated capsule polysaccharides | Hib, PCV13, meningococcal | Enhances response in young children via T-dependent mechanism | Complex to manufacture |

#### Historical Note
**Variolation**: Historical practice of deliberately inoculating individuals with smallpox material from infected persons; practiced since 10th-century China. Risky (could cause disease); replaced by safer vaccination.

**Edward Jenner** (1796): Observed cowpox immunity conferred protection against smallpox. Used cowpox material to vaccinate a boy, successfully protecting against subsequent smallpox exposure.

#### Emerging Vaccine Technologies
**DNA Vaccines**: Incorporate recombinant plasmids containing antigen genes → stimulate humoral and cellular immunity without active disease risks. Current applications include veterinary use; human use expanding (mRNA vaccines for COVID-19 represent similar principle).

#### Nursing Vaccination Applications
- **DTaP** (infants/children): Diphtheria toxoid, tetanus toxoid, acellular pertussis components
- **Tdap** (adolescents/adults, including pregnant women at 27–36 weeks): Reduced-dose tetanus, diphtheria, acellular pertussis
- **MMR**: Measles, mumps, rubella — live attenuated; 2 doses
- **Varicella**: Chickenpox — live attenuated; 2 doses
- **PCV13 + PPSV23**: Pneumococcal — infants + high-risk adults
- **Influenza**: Annual — inactivated (injection) or live attenuated (nasal)
- **HBV**: Hepatitis B — 3-dose series; all healthcare workers
- **Meningococcal**: MCV4 at age 11-12 + booster at 16
- **HPV**: Gardasil — 2-3 doses; up to age 26 routinely, up to 45 with shared decision making
- **Shingles (Zoster)**: Shingrix (RZV) — 2 doses; all adults ≥50 years; preferred over Zostavax
